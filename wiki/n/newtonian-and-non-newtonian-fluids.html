Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids

fluid-mechanicsfundamentalsviscositynon-newtonian

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Viscosity and Newton's Law of Viscosity

Non-Newtonian fluids

Fluids that do not obey the linear law τ∝du/dy of Newton's Law of Viscosity. Examples: blood, milk, polymer solutions, gelatin, starch suspensions, ink, and some high-molecular-weight oils. Because the stress-strain-rate relation isn't a fixed constant slope, μ is no longer a single material constant — instead an apparent viscosity is defined at each shear rate as the local slope/ratio τ/(du/dy), which itself varies with flow condition.

The power-law (Ostwald–de Waele) model

A common empirical model for the non-Newtonian relation:

τ=m(dydu​)n​

  • m — flow consistency index
  • n — flow behaviour index (dimensionless)
n Behaviour Apparent viscosity as shear rate increases
n<1 Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) decreases — e.g. paint, blood, ketchup
n=1 Newtonian (m=μ) constant
n>1 Dilatant (shear-thickening) increases — e.g. cornstarch-water suspension

Connecting to the Deborah number

Time-dependent (viscoelastic) non-Newtonian behavior — where the fluid's response depends not just on the instantaneous shear rate but on its own relaxation history — is classified by the Deborah Number De=λ/tflow​: a fluid can behave essentially Newtonian/liquid-like on slow timescales (De≪1) yet elastic/solid-like on fast timescales (De≫1) — the same physical substance, different regime, exactly as Knudsen Number reclassifies the same gas as continuum or free-molecular depending on the length scale of the problem rather than the substance itself.

Cross-domain: power-law fluids vs strain-hardening solids

The power-law exponent n here is the direct fluid-mechanics cousin of the nonlinear stress-strain exponent used in solid-mechanics plasticity models (e.g. the Ramberg-Osgood relation ε=σ/E+K(σ)n, not currently in the Strength of Materials vault but a natural extension of Stress-Strain Diagram and Hooke's Law's discussion of the post-yield, nonlinear portion of the stress-strain curve): both use a single dimensionless exponent to interpolate between "stiffens/thickens as loading increases" and "softens/thins as loading increases," and both reduce to the linear (Newtonian / Hookean) case at the exponent's neutral value.
