T8-Q1: Operating Principle of the Induction Machine

EE1100tutorial-solutionmachinesinduction-machineoperating-principle

Hub: Tutorial 8 - Induction Machines · Theory: Theory - Three-Phase Induction Machines · Next: T8-Q2 - The Revolving Field

Explain the operating principle of the induction machine.

Tests: electromagnetic induction and the Lorentz force in a machine, why the rotor must run slower than the field, motor versus generator operation.

Principle and why it applies

An induction machine converts electrical to mechanical energy by induction, with no electrical connection to the rotor. Three physical laws do all the work:

  1. A three-phase stator winding produces a rotating magnetic field (Q2), speed ns​=120f/P.
  2. Faraday's law of induction. The rotating field cuts the rotor conductors, so an emf e=Blvrel​ (equivalently e=−dΦ/dt) is induced in them. The rotor is short-circuited (cage bars joined by end rings, or a wound rotor closed through slip rings), so the emf drives rotor currents.
  3. Lorentz force. A conductor of length l carrying current i in a field B feels a force F=Bil. The sum over all rotor bars is a torque on the rotor.

Lenz's law fixes the direction: the torque acts to reduce the relative motion between rotor and field, i.e. it drags the rotor in the direction the field rotates.

Solution (the argument in words)

  1. The stator, fed from the three-phase supply, magnetises the machine and sets up the revolving field.
  2. At standstill the relative speed between field and rotor is the full ns​, so the induced rotor emf and current are largest, and a starting torque is produced.
  3. The rotor accelerates. As it speeds up the relative speed ns​−nr​ falls, so the emf, the rotor current and (beyond a point) the torque fall.
  4. It settles where the torque equals the load torque, at a speed nr​ that is slightly below ns​. It can never reach ns​: at that speed there would be no relative motion, hence no emf, no current and no torque. The fractional difference s=(ns​−nr​)/ns​ is the slip (Q4); this is why the machine is called asynchronous.
  5. Increase the load and the rotor slows a little: the slip grows, the rotor current and torque rise to meet the demand. The machine therefore self-regulates its torque with a small change of speed, and the stator current rises to supply the power (transformer action: the stator is the primary, the rotor a short-circuited secondary, 10 Single Phase Transformers).
  6. If the rotor is driven above ns​ by a prime mover the slip is negative, the torque reverses and the machine acts as an induction generator, returning power to the supply (it still takes its magnetising current from the grid).

Result

Rotating stator field → induced rotor emf and current (Faraday) → force on rotor conductors (Lorentz) → torque in the direction of the field (Lenz), with the rotor always running at nr​<ns​, s=(ns​−nr​)/ns​.

Check

Limiting cases: s=0⇒ torque 0; s=1 (standstill) ⇒ starting torque and a large starting current (5–7× rated); s<0⇒ generator. These match the shape of the torque–slip curve of T8-Q5 - Torque-Slip Characteristic.

Trap / alternative

  • "Induction" refers to the rotor: the stator is supplied directly and only the rotor current is induced.
  • Don't say the rotor "follows" the field at the same speed; that would be a synchronous machine, in which the rotor carries DC and locks onto the field.
  • The torque exists only because of relative motion: the rotor currents and field are stationary relative to each other only when both are described from the stator (Induction Motor - Slip, Rotor Circuit and Power Flow).

Concepts used
