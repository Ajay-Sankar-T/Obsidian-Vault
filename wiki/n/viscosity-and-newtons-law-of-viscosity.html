Viscosity and Newton's Law of Viscosity

fluid-mechanicsfundamentalsviscosity

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Fluids and Solids

Definition

Viscosity is a fluid property observed only when the fluid is in motion — it quantifies a fluid's internal resistance to the rate of shearing, exactly filling the role that a fluid cannot fill statically (see Fluids and Solids: no static shear resistance, but plenty of dynamic resistance).

Newton's law of viscosity

For simple parallel (shear) flow, with velocity u varying only in the direction y perpendicular to the flow:

τ=μdydu​​

  • τ — shear stress (force per unit area) between adjacent fluid layers
  • du/dy — velocity gradient (rate of shear strain) across the flow
  • μ — coefficient of (dynamic) viscosity, a fluid property with units Pa·s

This is the fluid-mechanics analogue of Hooke's law σ=Eε (Stress-Strain Diagram and Hooke's Law): both are linear constitutive laws relating a stress to a rate or amount of deformation, but Newton's law relates stress to a strain rate (du/dy, a rate of angular deformation γ˙​) rather than to strain itself — a solid's shear stress depends on how much it has been sheared, while a fluid's shear stress depends on how fast it is being sheared.

Newtonian fluids

Fluids that obey the linear relation τ∝du/dy, with μ constant for a given fluid at fixed temperature and pressure, regardless of the flow condition. Examples: water, air, oil, mercury. μ is a true material property, exactly like Young's modulus E is for a linear-elastic solid.

Kinematic viscosity

ν=ρμ​

units m²/s — appears naturally in the Reynolds number and diffusion-like terms in the full Navier-Stokes equations (not derived in this vault yet, but the natural next topic after Linear Momentum Equation).
