Theory: Balanced Three-Phase Circuits

EE1100tutorial-theoryAC-circuitsthree-phase

← up: Tutorial 4 · lectures: Lecture 23 Three-phase Circuits, Lecture 24 Three-phase Systems (Contd…), Lecture 25 Three-phase Systems - Examples · week: 09 Three Phase Circuits and Power Measurement · glossary: Three-Phase Systems

The idea in one paragraph

Three sinusoidal sources of equal magnitude, 120∘ apart, driving a balanced load, deliver constant instantaneous power and need no return current in the neutral. A balanced problem is solved by analysing one phase with ordinary single-phase phasors and writing the other two down by the ±120∘ symmetry; the connection (star or delta) only changes how line quantities relate to phase quantities.

1. Balanced sources and phase sequence

For the usual positive (abc) sequence, phase voltages (line to neutral):

Vˉan​=Vp​∠0∘,Vˉbn​=Vp​∠−120∘,Vˉcn​=Vp​∠+120∘,Vˉan​+Vˉbn​+Vˉcn​=0.

The vanishing sum is the reason a balanced system needs no neutral current, and why in Q10(c) the sum of the line currents is zero.

2. Star (Y) connection

Each load sits between a line and the neutral. Line current = phase current (IL​=Ip​). The line voltage is the difference of two phase voltages:

Vˉab​=Vˉan​−Vˉbn​=3​Vp​∠(θ+30∘),VL​=3​Vp​.

So in star the line voltages are 3​ larger than the phase voltages and lead the corresponding phase voltage by 30∘ (Q11, Q12).

3. Delta (Δ) connection

Each load sits between two lines. Line voltage = phase voltage (VL​=Vp​). The line current is the difference of two phase currents:

Iˉa​=Iˉab​−Iˉca​=3​Ip​∠(θab​−30∘),IL​=3​Ip​.

(Q9, Q10.) A balanced delta load ZΔ​ is equivalent, for the line quantities, to a star load ZY​=ZΔ​/3 (the same Y–Δ rule as for resistors, Theory - Circuit Laws and Network Theorems §10).

4. Power

For a balanced load with phase impedance angle ϕ (the angle between the phase voltage and phase current, i.e. the angle of Zp​):

P=3Vp​Ip​cosϕ=3​VL​IL​cosϕ,Q=3​VL​IL​sinϕ,S=3​VL​IL​=3Vp​Ip​.

The formula in line quantities holds for both star and delta, and ϕ is the load's impedance angle, not the angle between a line voltage and a line current (those differ by 30∘). P is constant in time, unlike single-phase power which pulsates at 2f.

5. Solution procedure

  1. Fix the connection of the load (and source) and the stated voltage (line-to-line unless said otherwise: "220 V three-phase" means VL​=220 V).
  2. Pick the reference (Vˉan​ or Vˉab​ at 0∘) and the sequence (abc).
  3. Phase impedance and phase current: Iˉp​=Vˉp​/Zp​.
  4. Line current from the connection rule of §2 or §3; use ±120∘ for the other phases.
  5. Power from §4. Check with P=3Ip2​Rp​.

Phasor diagrams for Q9, Q10 and Q12 are in T4-Q9 - Balanced Delta Load, T4-Q10 - Delta Load Power and T4-Q12 - Star Load Voltage and Current. For measuring P with two wattmeters see 09 Three Phase Circuits and Power Measurement.

6. Common traps

  • Using VL​ as the phase voltage in a star circuit (or IL​ as the phase current in a delta).
  • Using the angle between line quantities in cosϕ.
  • Treating "220 V" as a phase voltage; three-phase ratings are line-to-line.
  • Forgetting the 30∘ phase shift between line and phase quantities when asked for phasors.
  • Reporting phase currents when the question asks for line currents (a delta load has IL​=3​Ip​); see Q10.

7. Cross-domain links

  • Rotating Vectors - Phasors, Velocity Polygons and Complex Kinematics: three phasors 120∘ apart that sum to zero are the same geometry as the balanced rotating vectors that produce the revolving field of Revolving Magnetic Field and Synchronous Speed (Tutorial 8).
  • Conservation Laws and Flow Networks: a constant total from three pulsating powers that are 120∘ apart is a conservation-of-flow statement: the three ripples cancel exactly, as in three evenly phased pumps feeding one pipe.
