Lagrangian and Eulerian Descriptions

fluid-mechanicskinematics

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Continuum Concept

Lagrangian description

Follow individual fluid particles through space and time: x=x(t) for each labeled particle. Memory: Lagrangian = Follow.

Eulerian description

Observe the fluid at fixed locations in space, as a field: V=V(x,y,z,t). Memory: Eulerian = Observe.

Why fluid mechanics almost always uses Eulerian

Tracking every individual particle (Lagrangian) is natural for solid-body dynamics (a handful of rigid bodies) but impractical for a fluid continuum with effectively infinitely many "particles." The Eulerian field description is what makes the continuum assumption pay off: instead of tracking particles, define ρ,V,p as fields and write PDEs for them at fixed points.

The two descriptions are not independent

Even working in the Eulerian frame, physical laws (Newton's second law, conservation of mass for a fixed set of matter) are stated most naturally for a fixed body of material, i.e. Lagrangian. The material derivative is exactly the bridge: it computes "the rate of change following a particle" (D/Dt, Lagrangian) using only Eulerian field quantities and their spatial/temporal derivatives — never actually tracking any particle's trajectory. This same bridge — a "system" viewpoint (fixed matter) translated into a "field/region" viewpoint (fixed space) — reappears exactly as System and Control Volume's distinction, one level up (an entire body of fluid, rather than a single particle).
