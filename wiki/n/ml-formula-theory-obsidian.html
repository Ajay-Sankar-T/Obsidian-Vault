Machine Learning Formula Sheet — Theory and Intuition

How to use this note

Each item explains what the formula measures, why it matters, and how to interpret it. The formulas are written in LaTeX so Obsidian can render them. Unless stated otherwise, vectors are column vectors and sums run over the indicated observations.

1. Descriptive statistics

1. Mean (average)

μ=n1​i=1∑n​xi​

Idea: The arithmetic mean is the balance point of a dataset. Add all values and divide by the number of observations.

Use: Summarising a feature, filling missing values (when appropriate), and calculating variance or standardisation.

Caution: The mean is sensitive to outliers. For a sample, the symbol xˉ is often used; μ usually denotes a population mean.

2. Variance

σ2=n1​i=1∑n​(xi​−μ)2

Idea: Variance measures average squared distance from the mean. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling and penalises large deviations more heavily.

Interpretation: Larger variance means greater spread. Units are squared.

Caution: This is the population variance. For an unbiased sample-variance estimate, use s2=n−11​∑i​(xi​−xˉ)2.

3. Standard deviation

σ=n1​i=1∑n​(xi​−μ)2​

Idea: Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so it measures spread in the same units as the original feature.

Use: Understanding typical spread, z-scores, and spotting unusually distant values. A standard deviation of zero means every value is identical.

4. Covariance

cov(X,Y)=n1​i=1∑n​(xi​−μX​)(yi​−μY​)

Idea: Covariance indicates whether two variables tend to move together.

  • Positive: values above the mean of X tend to pair with values above the mean of Y.
  • Negative: one tends to be above its mean when the other is below.
  • Near zero: little linear co-movement; nonlinear relationships can still exist.

Caution: Its magnitude depends on the variables' units and scales. Sample covariance commonly uses n−1.

5. Correlation coefficient

ρXY​=σX​σY​cov(X,Y)​

Idea: Pearson correlation standardises covariance, producing a unitless measure of linear association between −1 and +1.

  • +1: perfect positive linear relationship.
  • −1: perfect negative linear relationship.
  • 0: no linear correlation.

Caution: Correlation does not establish causation and can miss nonlinear relationships. If either variable has zero variance, correlation is undefined.

2. Distances and feature scaling

6. Euclidean distance

d(x,y)=i=1∑n​(xi​−yi​)2​

Idea: Straight-line distance between two points in Euclidean space.

Use: Clustering, nearest-neighbour methods, and geometric comparisons. Large-scale features can dominate, so scaling may be necessary.

7. Manhattan distance

d(x,y)=i=1∑n​∣xi​−yi​∣

Idea: Sum of absolute coordinate differences; also called city-block or L1​ distance.

Use: Grid-like movement, robust-ish distance-based comparisons, and sparse/high-dimensional settings. It is less dominated by a single large coordinate difference than squared Euclidean distance.

8. Minkowski distance

dp​(x,y)=(i=1∑n​∣xi​−yi​∣p)1/p,p≥1

Idea: A family of distances. p=1 gives Manhattan distance; p=2 gives Euclidean distance.

Use: Adjusting how strongly large coordinate differences affect distance. The choice of p changes the geometry of "nearby."

9. Z-score standardisation

z=σx−μ​

Idea: Expresses a value in standard-deviation units relative to the mean.

Interpretation: z=2 means two standard deviations above the mean. Standardisation commonly gives a feature mean near zero and standard deviation near one.

Caution: Calculate mean and standard deviation on the training set only, then reuse them on validation/test data to avoid data leakage. If σ=0, the feature is constant and needs special handling.

10. Min--max normalisation

x′=xmax​−xmin​x−xmin​​

Idea: Linearly maps the observed minimum to 0 and maximum to 1.

Use: Keeping features in a fixed range, particularly when algorithms are sensitive to feature scale.

Caution: Outliers strongly affect the range. A constant feature makes the denominator zero. Fit min/max on training data only; future values may fall outside [0,1].

3. Neural-network activation functions

11. Sigmoid

σ(x)=1+e−x1​

Idea: Squashes any real input into (0,1).

Use: Commonly converts a binary-classification logit into a probability estimate.

Limitations: Saturates for very positive or negative inputs, producing small gradients (vanishing-gradient problem). It is not usually the default hidden-layer activation.

12. Hyperbolic tangent (tanh)

tanh(x)=ex+e−xex−e−x​

Idea: Squashes inputs into (−1,1) and is zero-centred.

Use: Historically common in hidden layers and recurrent networks.

Limitations: It also saturates for large ∣x∣, causing small gradients.

13. ReLU

f(x)=max(0,x)

Idea: Outputs zero for negative inputs and the input itself for positive inputs.

Use: A common hidden-layer activation because it is simple and does not saturate on the positive side.

Limitation: A unit can become "dead" if it remains in the negative region and receives zero gradient.

14. Leaky ReLU

f(x)={x,alphax,​x>0x≤0​

Idea: Like ReLU, but allows a small negative slope α for negative inputs.

Why: Provides a nonzero gradient on the negative side and may reduce dead ReLU units. The value of α is a design choice.

15. Softmax

softmax(zi​)=∑j=1K​ezj​ezi​​

Idea: Converts K logits into nonnegative values that sum to one.

Use: Multiclass classification, often paired with cross-entropy loss.

Caution: The outputs are a probability distribution over classes, but are not automatically well-calibrated probabilities. In practice, implementations subtract the largest logit before exponentiation for numerical stability.

4. Linear regression and loss functions

16. Linear model

y^​=wTx+b

Idea: Predicts a target as a weighted sum of input features plus an intercept (bias) b.

Interpretation: Each weight describes the model's change in prediction for a one-unit change in that feature, holding other features fixed.

Use: Regression and as a simple building block for neural networks. A linear model can still use transformed or engineered input features.

17. Mean squared error (MSE)

J=n1​i=1∑n​(yi​−y^​i​)2

Idea: Average squared prediction error.

Strength: Strongly penalises large errors and is differentiable, making it convenient for optimisation.

Limitation: Sensitive to outliers. MSE has squared target units.

18. Mean absolute error (MAE)

J=n1​i=1∑n​∣yi​−y^​i​∣

Idea: Average absolute prediction error.

Strength: Easier to interpret in the target's original units and less sensitive to large outliers than MSE.

Limitation: The absolute value is not differentiable at zero, though optimisation methods can use subgradients.

19. Gradient of linear-regression MSE

For a design matrix X with one row per observation and parameter vector w,

J(w)=n1​∥Xw−y∥22​

∇w​J=n2​XT(Xw−y)

Idea: The gradient points in the direction of steepest local increase in the loss.

Shape check: If X is n×d, then XT(Xw−y) has d entries, one per weight. If the model includes an intercept, it can be represented by a column of ones in X.

20. Gradient-descent update

wt+1​=wt​−η∇w​J(wt​)

Idea: Update parameters in the direction opposite the gradient to reduce the objective locally.

Role of learning rate η: Too small can make learning slow; too large can overshoot or diverge. Gradients may be computed using all examples, a mini-batch, or a single example.

5. Classification and probability

21. Logistic regression

P(y=1∣x)=1+e−(wTx+b)1​

Idea: Logistic regression applies sigmoid to a linear score (logit) to estimate the probability of the positive class.

Decision: With a 0.5 threshold, predict class 1 when the logit is nonnegative. The threshold can be changed to reflect different costs of false positives and false negatives.

Note: Despite its name, logistic regression is a classification model.

22. Binary log loss

J=−n1​i=1∑n​[yi​log(pi​)+(1−yi​)log(1−pi​)]

Idea: Penalises probability estimates that assign low probability to the true binary label.

Interpretation: Confident correct predictions incur low loss; confident incorrect predictions incur very high loss.

Caution: Use numerically stable implementations and avoid evaluating log(0) directly.

23. Multiclass cross-entropy loss

J=−n1​i=1∑n​k=1∑K​yik​log(pik​)

Idea: For one-hot labels, only the log-probability of the correct class contributes to each example's loss.

Use: Commonly paired with a softmax output for mutually exclusive classes. Some libraries expect raw logits and combine softmax with cross-entropy internally; do not apply softmax twice.

24. Naive Bayes

P(Ck​∣x)∝P(Ck​)j=1∏d​P(xj​∣Ck​)

Idea: Uses Bayes' rule with the conditional-independence assumption that features are independent given the class.

Terms: P(Ck​) is the prior; P(xj​∣Ck​) is a feature likelihood; the posterior is the updated class probability.

Why "naive": The independence assumption is often imperfect, but the model can still work well, especially for text classification. Products are commonly computed as sums of log-probabilities.

25. Bayes' theorem

P(A∣B)=P(B)P(B∣A)P(A)​

Idea: Updates belief in event A after observing evidence B.

  • P(A): prior.
  • P(B∣A): likelihood.
  • P(A∣B): posterior.
  • P(B): evidence/normalising probability.

Requirement: P(B)>0.

26. Prior probability

P(Ck​)

Idea: Probability of class Ck​ before considering the current example's features.

Example: If 10% of training emails are spam, the empirical spam prior is about 0.10.

Role: Affects predictions when classes are imbalanced or feature evidence is weak.

27. Likelihood

P(x∣Ck​)

Idea: How probable the observed features are under class Ck​.

Distinction: P(x∣Ck​) is not the same as P(Ck​∣x). The former is the likelihood of the evidence under a class; the latter is the posterior probability of the class given evidence.

28. Posterior probability

P(Ck​∣x)

Idea: Probability of class Ck​ after combining prior information with observed features.

Use: Classification can choose the class with the largest posterior, though application-specific decision costs may justify a different rule.

6. Regularisation and support vector machines

29. L2 regularisation (Ridge)

J=MSE+λ∥w∥22​

Idea: Adds a penalty proportional to the squared size of the weights.

Effect: Encourages smaller weights, often improving generalisation and reducing sensitivity to correlated features. Weights usually shrink toward zero but rarely become exactly zero.

Parameter: Larger λ means stronger regularisation. Bias/intercept terms are often excluded from the penalty.

30. L1 regularisation (Lasso)

J=MSE+λ∥w∥1​

where ∥w∥1​=∑j​∣wj​∣.

Idea: Penalises the absolute values of weights.

Effect: Can drive some weights exactly to zero, creating a sparse model and performing a form of feature selection.

Caution: With highly correlated features, which feature remains can be unstable.

31. Elastic Net

J=MSE+λ1​∥w∥1​+λ2​∥w∥22​

Idea: Combines L1 sparsity with L2 shrinkage.

Why: Can select features while behaving more stably than pure Lasso when predictors are correlated. Implementations may parameterise the overall strength and the L1/L2 mixture differently.

32. SVM margin

For a linear separator with weight vector w,

M=∥w∥2​2​

Idea: Under the canonical SVM scaling, the distance between the two margin boundaries wTx+b=+1 and wTx+b=−1 is 2/∥w∥.

Goal: Support Vector Machines seek a separating boundary with a large margin, which can improve generalisation.

Caution: This expression assumes the canonical margin scaling; multiplying w and b by a constant changes their numerical values but not the decision boundary.

33. SVM decision function

f(x)=sign(wTx+b)

Idea: The sign of the score assigns one of two classes. The boundary is wTx+b=0.

Note: The raw score's magnitude relates to distance from the boundary after scaling by ∥w∥; it is not automatically a calibrated probability.

34. Hinge loss

L=max(0,1−y(wTx+b)),y∈{−1,+1}

Idea: Gives zero loss when an example is correctly classified with a margin of at least one. Otherwise, it penalises insufficient margin or misclassification.

Use: Standard loss for a linear SVM, typically combined with a weight regularisation term.

35. Kernel trick

K(x,z)=ϕ(x)Tϕ(z)

Idea: A kernel computes an inner product in a transformed feature space without explicitly constructing ϕ(x).

Why: Enables linear algorithms in feature space to learn nonlinear boundaries in the original input space.

Requirement: Standard kernel SVM theory relies on a valid positive-semidefinite kernel.

36. Polynomial kernel

K(x,z)=(xTz+c)d

Idea: Represents interactions up to a polynomial degree controlled by d; c controls the contribution of lower-degree terms.

Use: Modelling nonlinear relationships with feature interactions without manually expanding all polynomial features.

Note: Some libraries use a scale factor as well as c and d.

37. RBF (Gaussian) kernel

K(x,z)=e−γ∥x−z∥22​

Idea: Similarity decreases exponentially with squared distance.

Role of γ: Large γ makes similarity highly local and can create complex boundaries; small γ makes similarity vary more smoothly.

Caution: Feature scaling is important. Some references write γ=1/(2σ2).

7. Similarity and classification metrics

38. Cosine similarity

sim(x,y)=∥x∥2​∥y∥2​xTy​

Idea: Measures the cosine of the angle between two nonzero vectors.

Interpretation: Near 1 means similar direction; 0 means perpendicular; near -1 means opposite direction.

Use: Text embeddings, document similarity, and high-dimensional sparse vectors. It ignores vector magnitude; undefined if either vector is zero.

39. Precision

Precision=TP+FPTP​

Idea: Of all examples predicted positive, what fraction really are positive?

Prioritise precision when: False positives are costly, such as flagging legitimate transactions as fraud.

Edge case: Undefined when TP+FP=0; libraries may define a convention.

40. Recall (sensitivity)

Recall=TP+FNTP​

Idea: Of all actual positive examples, what fraction did the model find?

Prioritise recall when: Missing a positive case is costly, such as failing to flag a dangerous defect.

Trade-off: Raising the decision threshold often increases precision and decreases recall, though not universally at every threshold.

41. F1-score

F1​=2P+RPR​

where P is precision and R is recall.

Idea: Harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is high only when both are reasonably high.

Use: Imbalanced classification when both false positives and false negatives matter.

Limitation: F1 ignores true negatives and depends on the chosen decision threshold.

42. Accuracy

Accuracy=TP+TN+FP+FNTP+TN​

Idea: Fraction of all predictions that are correct.

Limitation: Can be misleading for imbalanced datasets. If 99% of examples are negative, always predicting negative gives 99% accuracy but zero recall for the positive class.

8. Bias, variance, and generalisation

43. Bias

A common prediction-bias definition is

Bias(f^​(x))=E[f^​(x)]−f(x)

Idea: Bias measures the systematic difference between the model's average prediction over possible training sets and the true target function.

High bias: Often associated with an overly simple model that underfits. The image's shorthand "E[y^​]−y" conveys this idea, but f(x) makes the target function explicit.

44. Variance of an estimator

Var(f^​(x))=E[(f^​(x)−E[f^​(x)])2]

Idea: Measures how much predictions vary when the model is trained on different datasets.

High variance: Often associated with a model that is too sensitive to its training data and may overfit.

45. Bias--variance trade-off

For squared-error prediction under the usual assumptions,

E[(f^​(x)−y)2]=Bias(f^​(x))2+Var(f^​(x))+σϵ2​

Idea: Expected prediction error can be decomposed into squared bias, variance, and irreducible noise.

Trade-off: Increasing model complexity often reduces bias but increases variance. The objective is good performance on unseen data, not simply minimising training error.

Note: This decomposition is an idealised result under assumptions such as additive zero-mean noise; it is not a universal identity for every loss and setting.

9. Optimisation algorithms

46. Learning-rate decay

One schedule is

ηt​=1+ktη0​​

Idea: Gradually reduces the learning rate from initial value η0​; k controls how quickly it decays.

Why: Larger steps can help early progress, while smaller steps later can improve stability around a minimum.

Note: This is one possible schedule, not the only one.

47. Exponential decay

ηt​=η0​e−λt

Idea: Learning rate decreases by a multiplicative factor over time.

Parameter: Larger λ causes faster decay. A practical schedule should not reduce the learning rate so quickly that learning stalls prematurely.

48. Momentum update

A common momentum convention is

vt​=βvt−1​+(1−β)∇J(wt​)

wt+1​=wt​−ηvt​

Idea: Momentum smooths gradients over time, accumulating consistent directions and reducing oscillation in narrow valleys.

Notation caution: Some textbooks omit the (1−β) factor or define the velocity differently. These conventions are equivalent only with appropriate parameter rescaling.

49. Adam optimiser

A common Adam formulation is

mt​=β1​mt−1​+(1−β1​)gt​

vt​=β2​vt−1​+(1−β2​)(gt​⊙gt​)

Bias corrections:

m^t​=1−β1t​mt​​,v^t​=1−β2t​vt​​

Update:

wt+1​=wt​−ηv^t​​+ϵm^t​​

Idea: Adam combines momentum-like first-moment estimates with adaptive per-parameter scaling from second moments.

Terms: gt​ is the current gradient; ⊙ is elementwise multiplication; ϵ prevents division by zero.

Why bias correction: The moment estimates start at zero and are biased toward zero early in training. Typical defaults are β1​=0.9 and β2​=0.999, though tuning may help.

50. Matrix multiplication

For matrices A of size m×n and B of size n×p,

C=AB,Cij​=k=1∑n​Aik​Bkj​

Idea: Each output entry is the dot product of row i of A and column j of B.

Shape rule: The inner dimensions must match: (m×n)(n×p)=(m×p).

ML use: Dense layers, linear regression, attention calculations, and batches of vector operations. Matrix multiplication is generally not commutative: AB=BA.

11. Important notation

  • n: number of observations.
  • d: number of features.
  • K: number of classes.
  • xi​: observation/value; x: feature vector.
  • y: true target/label; y^​: prediction.
  • w: model weights; b: intercept/bias.
  • η: learning rate.
  • λ: regularisation strength (exact meaning varies by implementation).
  • TP: true positive; TN: true negative; FP: false positive; FN: false negative.
  • ∇J: gradient of objective/loss J.
  • E[⋅]: expectation; Var(⋅): variance.

12. Suggested learning order

  1. Statistics: mean, variance, standard deviation, covariance, correlation.
  2. Geometry and preprocessing: distances, z-score, min--max scaling.
  3. Prediction and optimisation: linear model, MSE/MAE, gradients, gradient descent.
  4. Classification: sigmoid/logistic regression, cross-entropy, Bayes' theorem, Naive Bayes.
  5. Generalisation: L1/L2/Elastic Net, bias--variance.
  6. Advanced classifiers: SVM, kernels, classification metrics.
  7. Neural-network training: activations, momentum, Adam, learning-rate schedules.
  8. Linear algebra foundation: vectors, norms, dot products, matrix multiplication.
Study method

For each formula, learn (1) what each symbol means, (2) what happens when the input/parameter increases, (3) one tiny numerical example, and (4) when the formula is appropriate. Memorising symbols without the assumptions is much less useful.
