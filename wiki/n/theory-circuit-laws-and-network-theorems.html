Theory: Circuit Laws and Network Theorems

EE1100tutorial-theoryDC-circuitsnetwork-theorems

← up: Tutorial 1 · lectures: Lecture 3 Kirchhoff_s Laws, Lecture 4 Mesh and Modal Methods for Circuit Analysis, Lecture 5 Node Pair Methods, Thevenin and Norton_s Theorems, Lecture 6 Thevenin, Norton, Maximum Power Transfer and Star-Delta Transformation, Lecture 7 Dependent Sources, Concept of Super Nodes, Super meshes · weeks: 01 Basic Concepts and Basic Laws, 02 Methods of Circuit Analysis, 03 DC Network Theorems

The one-paragraph version

A lumped circuit is a graph whose edges obey two kinds of equations: element laws (one per branch: v=Ri, or a source's value) and conservation laws (KCL at nodes, KVL around loops). Every analysis method in this tutorial is a way of choosing unknowns so that the two kinds of equations collapse into a small linear system. The network theorems then say that, because the system is linear, a complicated network can be replaced, as seen from two terminals, by one source and one resistor.

1. Variables and sign conventions

Quantity Symbol Meaning Unit
charge q C
current i=dq/dt rate of charge flow through a cross-section A
voltage vab​=Va​−Vb​ energy per unit charge to move +q from b to a V
power p=vi rate of energy transfer W

Passive sign convention. If the current i enters the terminal marked + of an element, then p=vi>0 means the element absorbs power. For a resistor this is automatic, p=i2R≥0. A source can have either sign: a battery being charged absorbs power, a dependent source can absorb too. Every "power delivered by a source" figure in this tutorial uses the opposite statement: delivered =vi with i leaving the + terminal.

all elements∑​pabsorbed​=0(Tellegen / conservation of energy)

This is the check used in Q3, Q4, Q12 and Q14: total delivered by the sources equals total absorbed by the resistors, with signs. It catches almost every sign error.

2. The two conservation laws

KCL (Kirchhoff's current law): ∑ileaving a node​=0. Why: a node is a point, it cannot store charge, so whatever flows in must flow out. (The same statement as ∇⋅J=0 in steady state, or fluid continuity at a pipe junction; see Conservation Laws and Flow Networks.)

KVL (Kirchhoff's voltage law): ∑varound a loop​=0. Why: the electric field in a lumped circuit is conservative, so the work done on a unit charge around any closed path is zero; equivalently every node has one well-defined potential.

Ohm's law v=Ri with R=ρL/A; conductance G=1/R.

Counting. A connected circuit with b branches and n nodes has n−1 independent KCL equations and b−n+1 independent KVL equations (the number of meshes if the circuit is planar). With the b element equations they fix the 2b unknowns (b currents and b voltages). Nodal analysis reduces the work to n−1 unknowns, mesh analysis to b−n+1.

3. Series, parallel, dividers

Rseries​=∑Rk​,R∥​1​=∑Rk​1​,R1​∥R2​=R1​+R2​R1​R2​​.

Voltage divider (same current through both): vk​=vRk​/∑R. Current divider (same voltage across both): i1​=iR2​/(R1​+R2​), the other resistor in the numerator. See Voltage Divider Rule and Series-Parallel Resistance Reduction; the R-2R ladder of Q1 is the cleanest example of reducing from the far end.

4. Sources

  • Ideal voltage source: fixes v; the current is whatever the rest of the circuit demands. Setting it to 0 V (a short circuit) is "turning it off".
  • Ideal current source: fixes i; the voltage is whatever the rest demands. Setting it to 0 A (an open circuit) is "turning it off".
  • Dependent (controlled) source: its value is a multiple of a voltage or current elsewhere (v=μvx​, i=βix​, v=rix​, i=gvx​), drawn as a diamond. It is not an independent excitation, so it can never be switched off in superposition, and it makes the Thevenin resistance non-trivial (Q4, Q5).

5. Nodal analysis

  1. Choose a reference node (ground); call the other node potentials V1​,…,Vn−1​.
  2. At each node write KCL with every branch current as (Vthis​−Vother​)/R.
  3. Solve the system GV=Is​. G is symmetric: diagonal = total conductance at the node, off-diagonal = minus the conductance between two nodes.

Supernode. A voltage source between two non-reference nodes has no defined branch current, so enclose it: treat the two nodes as one supernode, write one KCL for the whole group, and add the constraint Va​−Vb​=E. This is Q11, which has two supernodes at once.

Use nodal analysis when there are few nodes or many current sources.

6. Mesh analysis

  1. Draw one clockwise loop current per mesh (planar circuits).
  2. Write KVL around each mesh: ∑RIown​−∑Rshared​Ineighbour​=∑Erises​.
  3. A branch shared by two meshes carries the difference of the two mesh currents.

Supermesh. A current source shared by two meshes has an unknown voltage; remove it, write one KVL around the combined outer loop, and add the constraint Ia​−Ib​=Is​. A current source on the outside edge of one mesh simply fixes that mesh current (Q7, Q12).

Use mesh analysis when there are few meshes or many voltage sources.

7. Superposition

Because every element equation is linear, the response to several independent sources is the sum of the responses to each source acting alone, with the others turned off (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened). Dependent sources stay in every sub-circuit. It applies to currents and voltages but not to power (p=i2R is quadratic, so cross-terms survive). It is slower than nodal analysis when there are many sources, but it wins when the sources are of different kinds or you want each source's contribution (Q6).

8. Thevenin and Norton equivalents

Any linear two-terminal network is equivalent, as seen from its terminals, to a voltage source Vth​ in series with Rth​, or to a current source IN​ in parallel with RN​=Rth​, with

Vth​=voc​,IN​=isc​,Rth​=isc​voc​​.

Why it works: with a load attached the terminal relation is linear, v=Vth​−Rth​i, a straight line; two points (voc​ at i=0 and isc​ at v=0) fix it. Three ways to get Rth​:

  1. Turn off the independent sources and combine resistances as seen from the terminals (only valid when there are no dependent sources): Q8, Q9, Q10.
  2. Test source: with the independent sources off, connect vt​ (or it​) at the terminals and compute Rth​=vt​/it​. Always works and is needed with dependent sources (Q5).
  3. voc​/isc​.

Source transformation (E with R in series ↔ E/R with R in parallel) is the local version of the same equivalence (it is the hint for Tutorial 2, Q5).

9. Maximum power transfer

Load RL​ on a Thevenin source:

PL​=(Rth​+RL​)2Vth2​RL​​,dRL​dPL​​=0 ⇒ RL​=Rth​,Pmax​=4Rth​Vth2​​.

At that point the efficiency (power in the load over power from the source) is only 50 %: matching is for signals, where you want the most power, not for power systems, where you want efficiency. The curve is flat-topped, and PL​=Pmax​/2 has two solutions, from RL2​−6Rth​RL​+Rth2​=0, i.e. RL​=(3±22​)Rth​, one above and one below Rth​ (Q10).

10. Star–delta (Y–Δ) transformation

Two three-terminal resistor networks are equivalent if every pair of terminals sees the same resistance with the third terminal left open.

Δ→Y:  Ra​=Rab​+Rbc​+Rca​Rab​Rca​​  (product of the two adjacent),Y→Δ:  Rab​=Rc​Ra​Rb​+Rb​Rc​+Rc​Ra​​  (sum of products over the opposite).

For a balanced network RY​=RΔ​/3. Use it when a bridge has no series or parallel pair (Q13, Q14). The same transformation reappears in the three-phase loads of Tutorial 4 - Resonance, Filter and Three-Phase Circuits.

Choosing a method

The circuit has Prefer
few nodes, current sources nodal
few meshes, voltage sources mesh
a floating voltage source supernode
a current source shared by two meshes supermesh
one load whose value changes, or "what does this part look like" Thevenin / Norton
mixed sources, "contribution of each" superposition
a bridge with no series/parallel pair star–delta, or nodal
dependent sources nodal or mesh with the control variable written in terms of the unknowns; Thevenin by test source

Common traps

  • Getting the sign of a source wrong in a loop equation (write "rise" for −→+ in the direction of travel).
  • Switching off a dependent source in superposition or in a Thevenin calculation.
  • Quoting power for a source without saying delivered or absorbed.
  • Using the current divider with the wrong resistor in the numerator.
  • Forgetting that a shared branch carries I1​−I2​.
