Continuum Concept

fluid-mechanicsfundamentalscontinuum

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · governs validity of: Lagrangian and Eulerian Descriptions

The assumption

Fluid mechanics treats properties like pressure, velocity, temperature, and density as continuous functions of space and time — p(x,y,z,t), V(x,y,z,t) — rather than acknowledging that, at the molecular scale, a fluid is actually a swarm of discrete, colliding particles with enormous gaps between them. This is the continuum hypothesis: at each mathematical "point," properties are really local averages over a volume large enough to contain many molecules but small enough to be effectively a point on the macroscopic scale of the problem.

When it's valid

Valid whenever the mean free path of molecules is much smaller than the smallest length scale of the flow — this is exactly what Knudsen Number Kn=λ/L quantifies. For ordinary liquids and gases at everyday scales (Kn<0.01), the continuum assumption holds essentially perfectly; it breaks down for rarefied gases (very low pressure, e.g. high-altitude or vacuum-chamber flows) or for flows at extremely small length scales (micro/nanofluidics), where L shrinks toward λ.

Why every governing equation in this vault depends on it

The differential forms of the conservation laws — the differential continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t+∇⋅(ρV)=0 and the momentum equations used in Linear Momentum Equation — are only meaningful if ρ(x,y,z,t) and V(x,y,z,t) are differentiable fields. A discrete swarm of molecules has no well-defined derivative at a point; the continuum hypothesis is what licenses treating fluid mechanics as a subject of partial differential equations at all, in the same way the Concept of Stress note's stress field σ(x,y,z) implicitly assumes a solid continuum (no individual atoms/crystal grains resolved).
