Density, Specific Weight and Compressibility

conceptfluid-mechanicsfluid-propertiescompressibility

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Continuum Concept · next: Viscosity, Surface Tension

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: density ρ is mass per unit volume at a point, specific weight γ=ρg is weight per unit volume, and compressibility (bulk modulus K) measures how much the density changes when the pressure changes.

Intuition: pressing on a fluid squeezes it a little. A liquid barely gives, a gas gives a lot. Whether you may ignore that squeezing decides which set of equations you use for the whole problem (incompressible or compressible flow).

Governing Relations

The limit stops at a volume δV∗ that is still large compared with molecular spacing: that is the continuum hypothesis.

Ideal gas: p=ρRT with absolute p and absolute T (K); Rair​=287 J/(kg·K), k=cp​/cv​=1.4. For a gas, K=p (isothermal) or K=kp (isentropic).

Where:

  • ρ = density (kg/m³); γ = specific weight (N/m³); SG = specific gravity (dimensionless)
  • K = bulk modulus (Pa), the fluid analogue of Young's modulus for pure compression
  • a = speed of sound (m/s)

Assumptions: continuum; for K and a the process (isothermal or isentropic) must be stated.

Visual

No figure here: the content is a handful of numbers. The useful picture is the one in the differential continuity note: Dρ/Dt=−ρ∇⋅V, which is where density change enters the flow equations.

When To Use

  • Converting between mass-based and weight-based formulas: pressure from depth is p=γh=ρgh.
  • Deciding incompressible or compressible: compute Ma=V/a. If Ma≲0.3 the density change is below about 5 % and the flow is treated as incompressible. Almost all liquid flows qualify.
  • Water hammer, sound and shock problems, where K sets the wave speed.

Recognition pattern: if the problem gives a pressure rise in a liquid and asks for volume change, think of K. If it gives a gas at a state, think of p=ρRT.

Method

  1. Identify whether the fluid is a gas (use the ideal gas law with absolute p, T) or a liquid (use a tabulated ρ, correct for temperature if the range is large).
  2. Choose the compressibility test: Ma<0.3 for flow; Δp/K≪1 for a liquid under a pressure change.
  3. Apply: ΔV/V=−Δp/K, or Δρ/ρ≈Ma2/2 at a stagnation point.
  4. Check: units (kg/m³ against N/m³), absolute temperature, and that SG is a pure number.

Derivation / Why It Works

The density change at a stagnation point follows from Bernoulli. A flow decelerated to rest raises its pressure by about 21​ρV2, and Δρ≈Δp/a2 for a small change. Hence

ρΔρ​≈ρa221​ρV2​=2Ma2​.

At Ma=0.3 this is 4.5%, which is the origin of the "incompressible below 0.3" rule (the same limit appears in the differential kinematics bank).

Common Traps

  • Trap: calling a liquid "incompressible" and then using K=∞ for a sound-speed or water-hammer question.
  • Why: the flow is incompressible, but the fluid is not; K is large, not infinite, and a=K/ρ​ is finite.
  • Avoid it by: separating "incompressible flow" (a statement about ∇⋅V) from "incompressible fluid" (a statement about K).
  • Trap: using gauge pressure or °C in p=ρRT.
  • Avoid it by: converting to absolute pressure and kelvin first.
  • Trap: confusing ρ (kg/m³) with γ (N/m³) when a formula quotes one of them.

Example

Given

Air at 20 °C and 101.325 kPa. Also: water compressed from the surface to a depth of 1 km (Δp≈100 atm =10.13 MPa).

Solution

ρair​=287×293.15101325​=1.204 kg/m3,a=1.4×287×293.15​=343 m/s.

VΔV​=−2.2×10910.13×106​=−0.46%.

Answer: air 1.204 kg/m³ and 343 m/s; water shrinks by only 0.46 % at 1 km depth. Key takeaway: even 100 atm hardly compresses water, so treating water as incompressible costs almost nothing, while air changes by about 5 % already at Ma=0.3.

Quick Recall

In one line: ρ = mass/volume, γ=ρg, K=ρdp/dρ measures stiffness, a=K/ρ​, and flow is incompressible when Ma<0.3.

Formula / trigger:

VΔV​=−KΔp​,agas​=kRT​

Remember: water K≈2.2 GPa, air at 1 atm Ks​=142 kPa; a gas is about 104 times softer.
