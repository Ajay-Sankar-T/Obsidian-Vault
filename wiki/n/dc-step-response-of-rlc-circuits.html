What "DC step response" means

A DC source is suddenly switched on (or a switch changes the circuit configuration) at t=0, and the source value is constant thereafter. The response is entirely: initial condition → transient (natural response, shaped by α,ωn​) → constant final value.

x(t)=x∞​+(x0​−x∞​)e−t/τ  (first order)​x(t)=x∞​+xn​(t)  (second order, xn​ from the damping-case table)​

The graph

(left panel) — the classic single-exponential rise to a constant final value x∞​, from Week 4's three-value recipe. For a second-order (RLC) DC step, see the damping-case graphs in Series RLC Natural Response and Parallel RLC Natural Response — same idea, just the transient can now overshoot and ring before settling, which a first-order RC/RL circuit can never do (a first-order system has only one time constant and no oscillatory mode).

Why DC step response can never oscillate for first-order, but can for second-order

A first-order circuit's characteristic equation is linear in s (s+1/τ=0) — exactly one real root, exactly one exponential mode, no possibility of a complex-conjugate pair and hence no oscillation. A second-order circuit's characteristic equation (s2+2αs+ωn2​=0) can have a complex-conjugate root pair whenever ζ<1, and it's precisely that complex pair that produces the cosωd​t,sinωd​t oscillatory terms. This is why ringing is fundamentally a two-(or-more)-storage-element phenomenon — you need at least one L and one C (or two of the same kind coupled) for a circuit to be capable of oscillating at all, DC or AC.

The DC step procedure (recap, generalized to second order)

  1. Find x(0+) from continuity (vC​(0+)=vC​(0−), iL​(0+)=iL​(0−)).
  2. Find x(∞) by re-solving the circuit in true DC steady state (capacitor → open, inductor → short — valid here because the source is genuinely DC, unlike the AC case in AC Transient and Steady-State Response).
  3. Find α,ωn​ (or τ for first order) from the circuit's own R,L,C.
  4. Classify the damping case and write the matching template from the damping-case notes.
  5. Apply initial conditions to solve for the remaining constants.
