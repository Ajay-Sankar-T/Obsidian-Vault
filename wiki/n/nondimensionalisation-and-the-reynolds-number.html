Nondimensionalisation and the Reynolds Number

conceptfluid-mechanicsviscous-flowreynolds-numberscaling

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Navier-Stokes Equations, Important Dimensionless Groups · formulas: Similitude Formula Bank §6

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: scaling the Navier–Stokes equations with a length L and speed U shows that the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, the Reynolds number Re=ρUL/μ, is the single parameter (with Froude and Strouhal) that controls the flow.

Intuition: the equations have six dimensional quantities, but once you measure lengths in units of L and speeds in units of U, only one coefficient is left in front of the viscous term: 1/Re. Large Re: viscosity is a thin correction (boundary layers). Small Re: inertia is negligible (creeping flow).

Governing Relations

Define x∗=x/L, V∗=V/U, t∗=tU/L, p∗=(p−p∞​)/(ρU2). The incompressible Navier–Stokes equation becomes

∂t∗∂V∗​+(V∗⋅∇∗)V∗=−∇∗p∗−Fr1​k^+Re1​∇∗2V∗​,∇∗⋅V∗=0

with Re=μρUL​=νUL​ and Fr=gLU2​. If the flow oscillates at frequency ω use t∗=ωt and a coefficient St=ωL/U in front of the time derivative.

Meaning of Re (three equivalent readings):

Re=μU/L2ρU2/L​=viscous forceinertial force​=L/UL2/ν​=convection timediffusion time​

Limits:

Regime Condition Result
Creeping (Stokes) flow Re≪1 pressure scale μU/L;  0=−∇p+μ∇2V, linear, reversible
Laminar, viscous-dominated Re∼1–103 full equation; exact solutions where unidirectional
High-Re Re≫1 inviscid outer flow plus thin boundary layer, thickness δ/L∼Re−1/2 (laminar)
Turbulence above critical Re instabilities grow; see Laminar and Turbulent Flow

Boundary-layer estimate: balance inertia U2/L with viscous diffusion νU/δ2: δ/L∼(ν/UL)1/2=Re−1/2. For a flat plate the Blasius result is δ≈5x/Rex​​ (laminar).

Typical values:

Flow Re
swimming bacterium 10−5–10−4
person walking in air (0.5 m, 1.5 m/s) 5×104
water in a 25 mm pipe at 2 m/s 5×104
airliner wing (5 m chord, 250 m/s) 8×107
ship hull (100 m, 10 m/s) 109

Where: L,U = characteristic length and speed; ν=μ/ρ.

Assumptions: incompressible, constant properties; the choice of L,U is a convention and must be stated (pipe diameter against radius, mean against maximum velocity).

When To Use

  • Deciding which terms in Navier–Stokes can be neglected (inertia at low Re, viscosity outside boundary layers).
  • Setting up model tests: which groups to match.
  • Estimating boundary-layer thickness, entry length, or transition.

Recognition pattern: "which term dominates", "very slow viscous flow", "thin layer near the wall", "dynamic similarity" → scale the equations, find Re.

Method

  1. Identify characteristic L, U (and ω if unsteady) from the geometry and the flow.
  2. Choose the pressure scale: ρU2 for high Re, μU/L for low Re.
  3. Apply: divide every term; read off the coefficients St, 1/Fr, 1/Re.
  4. Check: drop terms with a small coefficient only if the neglected term does not control the boundary conditions (dropping the viscous term at high Re removes the ability to impose no-slip: a boundary layer fixes that).

Derivation / Why It Works

Substitute the scaled variables into ρ(∂t​V+V⋅∇V)=−∇p+ρg+μ∇2V. Each term acquires a dimensional factor: inertia ρU2/L, pressure ρU2/L, gravity ρg, viscous μU/L2. Divide by ρU2/L: inertia and pressure get coefficient 1, gravity gL/U2=1/Fr and viscous μ/(ρUL)=1/Re. Since the scaled equation and boundary conditions contain only Re, Fr and the scaled geometry, the scaled solution depends only on those: precisely the statement of the Π theorem, derived from the physics.

Common Traps

  • Trap: quoting Re without saying what L and U are.
  • Avoid it by: stating them (pipe: mean velocity and diameter, ReD​=VD/ν).
  • Trap: dropping 1/Re completely at high Re and then trying to apply no-slip.
  • Trap: using ρU2 as the pressure scale at Re≪1 (the pressure then scales as μU/L).
  • Trap: reading critical Re values as exact thresholds.

Example

Given

Air (ν=1.5×10−5 m²/s) flows at U=3 m/s over a flat plate. Find the Reynolds number at x=0.5 m and the laminar boundary-layer thickness there.

Solution

Rex​=νUx​=1.5×10−53(0.5)​=105,δ≈Rex​​5x​=3165(0.5)​=7.9 mm.

Answer: Rex​=105 (laminar, below the usual 5×105 transition) and δ≈7.9 mm, which is δ/x=1.6%: thin, as the Re−1/2 scaling predicts. Key takeaway: at high Re the viscous effect is confined to a layer of thickness ∼L/Re​; outside it the flow is nearly inviscid.
