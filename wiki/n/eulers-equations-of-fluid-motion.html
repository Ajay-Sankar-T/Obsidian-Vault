Euler's Equations of Fluid Motion

conceptfluid-mechanicsinviscid-floweuler-equations

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Material Derivative, Hydrostatic Equation, Ideal Fluid · formulas: Formula Bank §1

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: Euler's equations are Newton's second law for a frictionless fluid element: mass times acceleration equals the pressure force plus the body force, ρDV/Dt=−∇p+ρg.

Intuition: without viscosity the only surface force on an element is the pressure difference across it. A net pressure gradient accelerates the fluid toward lower pressure; gravity adds its weight. Everything about inviscid flow follows from this balance.

Governing Relation

ρDtDV​=ρ[∂t∂V​+(V⋅∇)V]=−∇p+ρg​

Cartesian (z up):  ρax​=−∂x∂p​,  ρay​=−∂y∂p​,  ρaz​=−∂z∂p​−ρg.

Streamline coordinates (s along the streamline, n toward the centre of curvature, radius R):

∂t∂V​+V∂s∂V​=−ρ1​∂s∂p​−g∂s∂z​,RV2​=−ρ1​∂n∂p​−g∂n∂z​

  • Along s: integrates to Bernoulli.
  • Normal to s: pressure increases away from the centre of curvature (centripetal force); for straight parallel streamlines the pressure is hydrostatic across the flow.

Rigid-body motion (DV/Dt=a uniform, no relative motion): ∇p=ρ(g−a), the hydrostatic equation with g replaced by g−a.

Where: p = pressure; g = gravitational acceleration; a=DV/Dt.

Assumptions: inviscid (μ=0), continuum, no body force other than gravity (add others as needed). Valid for compressible or incompressible flow.

Visual

Draw a curved streamline with a small element: pressure is higher on the outer face than the inner face; the difference provides the centripetal force ρV2/R per unit volume. For a flow in a duct bend, this gives high pressure on the outer wall and low on the inner wall.

When To Use

  • Deriving Bernoulli (integrate along a streamline) and the pressure field in an accelerating or rotating fluid.
  • Tank on a truck, rotating cylinder of liquid, bend pressure distribution.
  • Giving the inviscid limit of the Navier–Stokes equations: Navier-Stokes Equations with μ=0.

Recognition pattern: "frictionless", "ideal fluid", "tank accelerates", "liquid in a rotating container" → Euler's equations.

Method

  1. Identify the acceleration of each element (DV/Dt; zero relative acceleration in rigid-body cases).
  2. Choose coordinates (Cartesian for linear acceleration, streamline or cylindrical for curved motion).
  3. Apply ∇p=ρ(g−a) or the streamwise and normal components; integrate to find p.
  4. Check: reduce to hydrostatics when a=0; compare the sign of ∂p/∂n with the centripetal direction.

Derivation / Why It Works

For an element of volume dV the net pressure force is −∇pdV (pressure acts inward on every face; the difference across opposite faces gives the gradient). Add the body force ρgdV and set it equal to mass times acceleration ρdVDV/Dt. Dropping τ from the full momentum equation (Navier-Stokes) gives the same result. Using the vector identity (V⋅∇)V=∇(21​V2)−V×ζ, the equation becomes

∂t∂V​+∇(2V2​)−V×ζ=−ρ∇p​−g∇z.

The term V×ζ is perpendicular to V, so dotting with a displacement along a streamline eliminates it (Bernoulli), and it vanishes everywhere if the flow is irrotational.

Common Traps

  • Trap: using the hydrostatic equation ∇p=ρg in an accelerating tank.
  • Avoid it by: replacing g with g−a.
  • Trap: getting the sign of the normal pressure gradient wrong in curved flow.
  • Avoid it by: remembering that pressure rises away from the centre of curvature.
  • Trap: applying Euler's equations where viscosity matters (boundary layers, wakes, pipes at low Re).
  • Trap: forgetting that along a streamline Bernoulli applies, but between streamlines only if irrotational.

Example

Given

A tank of water accelerates horizontally at a=3 m/s². A cylindrical tank rotates at Ω=10 rad/s with water at rest relative to the walls.

Solution

Accelerating tank: ∇p=ρ(g−a) so ∂p/∂x=−ρa, ∂p/∂z=−ρg. The free surface is a line of constant p, with slope

tanθ=ga​=9.813​=0.306 ⇒ θ=17.0∘.

Rotating tank: ∂p/∂r=ρΩ2r, ∂p/∂z=−ρg, so the surface is z=Ω2r2/(2g). At r=0.1 m the rise above the axis is

z=2(9.81)102(0.1)2​=0.051 m.

Answer: surface tilted at 17.0°; paraboloid rising 5.1 cm at r=0.1 m. Key takeaway: when the fluid moves as a rigid body, replace g by g−a and the hydrostatic method still works.
