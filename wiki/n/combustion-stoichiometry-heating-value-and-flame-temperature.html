Combustion — Stoichiometry, Heating Value and Flame Temperature

conceptthermal-engineering
In one breath

Combustion is a chemical reaction treated with mass balances (how much air per kg of fuel), the first law (how much energy comes out) and one more idea: the adiabatic flame temperature, the temperature the products reach if all the released energy goes into heating them.

Part of Foundations of Thermal Engineering - MOC. Previous: Otto, Diesel and Dual Cycles and Engine Performance. Next: Turbomachines - Euler Equation and Velocity Triangles.

1. Stoichiometry

Air is taken as 21 % O₂ and 79 % N₂ by mole: 3.76 mol N₂ per mol O₂, so 4.76 mol air per mol O₂, with a mean molar mass 28.97 kg/kmol. Balance atoms for complete combustion to CO₂ and H₂O:

CH4​+2(O2​+3.76N2​)→CO2​+2H2​O+7.52N2​

C8​H18​+12.5(O2​+3.76N2​)→8CO2​+9H2​O+47N2​

Air–fuel ratio (mass): AF=nfuel​Mfuel​nair​Mair​​. Methane: 2(4.76)(28.97)/16.04=17.2; octane: 12.5(4.76)(28.97)/114.2=15.1 kg air per kg fuel (the stoichiometric or theoretical values). More air than that is excess air; the equivalence ratio is ϕ=AFstoich​/AFactual​: ϕ<1 lean, >1 rich. The dry-product analysis (Orsat) tells the actual air supply: measured CO₂ and O₂ in the exhaust fix the excess air.

2. First law and heating value

For a steady-flow burner with products leaving at the reactant temperature, the heat released is q=hR​−hP​=−ΔHcomb∘​ with enthalpies of formation: ΔH=∑P​nhf∘​−∑R​nhf∘​.

Species hf∘​ (kJ/mol)
CO₂ −393.52
H₂O (gas) −241.83
H₂O (liquid) −285.83
CH₄ (g) −74.87
C₈H₁₈ (l) −249.90
  • Higher heating value (HHV): water in the products condensed. Lower heating value (LHV): water as vapour (the one used for engines, since exhaust is hot).
  • Methane: LHV=393.52+2(241.83)−74.87=802.3 kJ/mol=50.0 MJ/kg; HHV=890.3 kJ/mol (the difference is 2hfg​).
  • Octane (liquid): LHV=8(393.52)+9(241.83)−249.90=5075 kJ/mol =44.4 MJ/kg.

For reactants and products at a different temperature, add the sensible enthalpies: h=hf∘​+(h(T)−h(298)).

3. Adiabatic flame temperature

With no heat loss and no work, HR​=HP​: the heat released heats the products:

P∑​ni​[hf,i∘​+Δhi​(Tad​)]=R∑​ni​hf,i∘​ ⟹ P∑​ni​Δhi​(Tad​)=LHV (per mol of fuel, reactants at 298 K)

Solve for Tad​ with temperature-dependent specific heats. (Property data, e.g. from the ideal-gas part of the CO₂, H₂O and N₂ equations of state in Tools/fte_core.py.)

Mixture (methane–air, air at 298 K) Tad​
stoichiometric (ϕ=1) 2327 K
43 % excess air (ϕ=0.7) 1846 K
100 % excess air (ϕ=0.5) 1481 K

Excess air dilutes the heat across more nitrogen and cools the flame: gas turbines run lean for exactly this reason (turbine-inlet temperature limits). The real stoichiometric flame is lower, about 2230 K, because of dissociation (CO₂ → CO + ½O₂, H₂O → H₂ + ½O₂, which absorb energy at high temperature) and heat loss; the peak temperature is near ϕ≈1.05.

4. Practical consequences

  • Complete combustion needs enough air and good mixing; deficiency gives CO and soot.
  • Rich or lean limits (flammability limits) bound the mixture that will burn at all; SI engines operate near ϕ=1, diesels are lean overall.
  • High flame temperature raises NOₓ (thermal NO forms above about 1800 K), so lean-burn or exhaust-gas recirculation reduces it.
  • Energy balance on an engine or boiler: fuel energy = work + exhaust loss + cooling and radiation loss.
Traps
  • Using air as pure oxygen: remember the 3.76 mol of N₂ that comes with every mole of O₂.
  • Mixing mass and molar air–fuel ratios.
  • Using HHV in an engine efficiency without saying so (LHV is the convention).
  • Forgetting that excess air changes the product mix (extra O₂ and N₂), not just the amount of air.
  • Taking the adiabatic flame temperature as the real one: heat loss and dissociation lower it.
Predict first: burn methane with 100 % excess air instead of stoichiometric. Will the flame be half as hot?

No: 1481 K against 2327 K. The temperature rise above 298 K falls from 2029 K to 1183 K (−42 %), not by half, because the same 802 kJ now heats 20 mol of products instead of 10.5 mol (the heat capacity of the products nearly doubles, while a hotter gas has a higher cp​ per mole, which softens the fall).

Why is the LHV of methane used for a gas turbine and not the HHV?

The exhaust leaves hot and the water stays as vapour: its latent heat is never recovered, so the usable heat is the lower value (802 against 890 kJ/mol).
