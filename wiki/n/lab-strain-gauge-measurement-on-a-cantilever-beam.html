Lab - Strain Gauge Measurement on a Cantilever Beam

conceptapplied-mechanics-labAM2540strength-of-materialsstrain-gauge
The experiment in one line

Bond a foil strain gauge at the root of a cantilever, hang weights from the free end, read the strain from a Wheatstone bridge and convert it to bending stress with σ=Eε; compare with σ=6PL/bt2. The skill is the bridge arrangement: one gauge reads temperature as well as strain, two gauges in the right arms read only strain, and twice as much of it.

Aim and apparatus

Aim: determine the bending stress produced in a cantilever by a transverse point load at its free end, using strain gauges. Apparatus: a steel or aluminium cantilever clamped at one end; foil strain gauges (typically R=120 Ω, gauge factor ≈2) bonded at the root, on the top and bottom faces; a Wheatstone-bridge board with a dummy gauge or completion resistors; excitation supply and a strain indicator or millivoltmeter; hanger and weights; vernier calipers.

Theory

Gauge factor. A foil grid changes resistance with strain,

GF=εΔR/R​,ΔR=GFRε.

GF≈2 for most metal foils. For ε=1000 με and R=120 Ω, ΔR=0.24 Ω (0.2 %): too small to read directly, so the gauge goes in a bridge.

Cantilever. With the load P at a distance L from the gauge, the bending moment at the gauge is M=PL. The surface stress and strain are

σ=IMc​=bt26PL​,ε=Eσ​=Ebt26PL​,

with b the width and t the thickness. The gauge sits near the root because M is largest there and the strain is the biggest signal; it must be far enough from the clamp to avoid the end effect. The gauge reads the strain along its grid, so it is aligned with the beam axis (the direction of the bending stress, a uniaxial state at the surface).

Wheatstone bridge. Four arms R1​…R4​ in sequence round a diamond, excitation Vex​ across one diagonal, output Vo​ across the other. For small changes

Vo​=4Vex​​GF(ε1​−ε2​+ε3​−ε4​),

where εi​ is the strain (and the apparent strain from temperature) seen by the gauge in arm i. Neighbouring arms subtract; opposite arms add.

Figure: the three standard arrangements. Quarter bridge with a dummy gauge: one active gauge, one unstrained gauge of the same type at the same temperature in the adjacent arm. Half bridge: for bending, one gauge on top (tension) and one underneath (compression). Full bridge: four active gauges (load cells).

Arrangement Output Temperature Notes
One gauge, no dummy Vex​GFε/4 not compensated thermal output adds to the strain signal
Quarter bridge + dummy (adjacent arm) Vex​GFε/4 cancels needs an identical unloaded gauge on the same material at the same temperature
Half bridge, top and bottom gauges in adjacent arms Vex​GFε/2 cancels doubles the signal; the preferred arrangement for a bending beam
Full bridge (two top, two bottom) Vex​GFε cancels four times the quarter bridge; also cancels axial strain

Why temperature cancels. A temperature change ΔT changes each gauge's resistance by the same ΔRT​ (the grid's own coefficient, plus the mismatch between grid and beam expansion). Equal changes in adjacent arms cancel in ε1​−ε2​; the bending strain is +ε on top and −ε underneath, so it adds.

Procedure (condensed)

  1. Measure b, t, the distance L from the gauge to the load point; note E (steel ≈200 GPa, aluminium ≈70 GPa).
  2. Connect the gauges into the chosen bridge; balance (zero) the output with the hanger on.
  3. Add weights in steps; read the output after each, waiting for it to settle; unload in the same steps.
  4. Convert: strain from the output, stress from σ=Eε; compare with 6PL/bt2.

Data reduction

Strain from the output: quarter ε=Vex​GF4Vo​​, half ε=Vex​GF2Vo​​ (per gauge), full ε=Vex​GFVo​​. Then σexp​=Eε and the percentage difference from 6PL/bt2. The slope of Vo​ against P is constant: its slope gives the calibration of the beam as a load cell.

Worked example

🔬 Illustrative: steel cantilever 25×6 mm, P=20 N at L=300 mm from the gauge, R=120 Ω, GF=2.1, Vex​=5 V

σ=25(6)26(20)(300)​=40 MPa, so ε=20000040​=200 με. ΔR=GFRε=2.1(120)(200×10−6)=0.0504 Ω. Quarter bridge: Vo​=45(2.1)(200×10−6)​=0.525 mV. Half bridge: 1.05 mV. Full bridge: 2.10 mV. The signals are millivolts: they need an amplifier (a strain indicator), and lead-wire resistance and thermal EMFs matter. Check: peak stress 40 MPa is far below yield, so the linear relation holds ✓.

🎯 Predict first: the single gauge at the root reads +50 με after the room warms by 5 °C with no load. Is the beam stressed?

No. That is apparent strain: the gauge resistance changes with temperature (and the grid and beam expand differently). For an uncompensated gauge on steel the thermal output is of the order of 10 με per °C (self-temperature-compensated gauges cut this to 1–2), so 5 °C can mimic 50 με, which would read as 200000×50×10−6=10 MPa of stress. A dummy gauge or a half bridge removes it.

⚠️ Errors and their size
Source Effect
Temperature drift with one gauge apparent strain, tens of με per °C
Gauge misalignment by θ reads ε(cos2θ−νsin2θ): error below 0.3 % for θ<3∘
Wrong L (distance to the gauge, not to the clamp) σ∝L: a 5 mm slip in 300 mm is 1.7 %
Thickness measured once σ∝1/t2: a 0.1 mm error on 6 mm is 3.3 %
Lead-wire resistance, poor zero offsets and a reduced gauge factor; use three-wire hook-up
Self-heating (high excitation) drift; use the lowest excitation that gives a clean signal

Questions (with answers)

1. What modification makes the result independent of temperature, and why?

Add a dummy gauge (same type, same material, unstrained, same temperature) in the adjacent arm, or better mount a second active gauge on the underside and wire both in adjacent arms (half bridge). Temperature changes both gauges equally; in adjacent arms the equal changes cancel, while the bending strain, being +ε and −ε on the two faces, adds. The half bridge also doubles the output.

2. Define the gauge factor.

The ratio of the fractional change in resistance to the applied strain, GF=(ΔR/R)/ε. It is a constant of the gauge, about 2 for metal foil (the sum of the geometric change, 1+2ν, and the change of resistivity). Semiconductor gauges have GF≈100 or more but are temperature sensitive.

3. Why is the gauge fixed near the root of the cantilever and aligned with its axis?

The bending moment M=PL is greatest at the root, giving the largest strain and best signal-to-noise; the surface stress there is uniaxial along the beam axis, which is the direction the gauge senses.

4. How would you also measure Poisson's ratio with the same beam?

Add a second gauge perpendicular to the first on the same face. The ratio of the transverse to the axial reading gives ν=−εt​/εa​ (for a beam in bending with a narrow section the surface state is nearly uniaxial). In a half bridge, with the two gauges in adjacent arms, the output is (1+ν) times a quarter-bridge signal.
