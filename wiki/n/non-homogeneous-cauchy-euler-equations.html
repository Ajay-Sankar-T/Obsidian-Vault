Non-homogeneous Cauchy-Euler Equations

Related: Cauchy-Euler Equations · Variation of Parameters · Undetermined Coefficients

ax2y′′+bxy′+cy=f(x),x>0.

As with any linear ODE: y=yc​(x)+yp​(x), where yc​ is found as in Cauchy-Euler Equations (three cases), and yp​ is any particular solution.

Two Techniques for yp​

Technique A — Variation of Parameters: works for any continuous f(x). Write in standard form y′′+axb​y′+ax2c​y=ax2f(x)​=:g(x). With y1​,y2​ the complementary solutions and W(x) their Wronskian:

yp​=−y1​∫W(x)y2​g(x)​dx+y2​∫W(x)y1​g(x)​dx

For Cauchy–Euler equations, W(x) is always a simple power of x, often making the integrals tractable.

Technique B — Transform t=lnx, then Undetermined Coefficients: works when f(x) becomes a "nice" function of t. E.g. f(x)=xk becomes f(et)=ekt (UC-friendly); f(x)=xklnx becomes tekt. Solve for yp​(t) using Undetermined Coefficients, then substitute back t=lnx. Usually faster when f(x) is a power of x (with or without lnx).

Worked Examples

Example — Technique B (power of x)

Solve x2y′′−2xy′−4y=x3, x>0. From Cauchy-Euler Equations, yc​=C1​x4+C2​x−1.

Transform: y¨​−y˙​−2y˙​−4y=e3t⇒y¨​−3y˙​−4y=e3t. AE roots 4,−1 match yc​. Since 3 is not a root, try yp​=Ae3t: 9A−9A−4A=−4A=1⇒A=−41​. So yp​(t)=−41​e3t, and since e3t=x3:

yp​(x)=−41​x3,y=C1​x4+C2​x−1−41​x3

Example — Technique A (with lnx forcing)

Solve x2y′′−2xy′+2y=x3lnx, x>0. AE m(m−1)−2m+2=0⇒m2−3m+2=0⇒(m−1)(m−2)=0. Roots 1,2: yc​=C1​x2+C2​x.

Standard form: y′′−x2​y′+x22​y=xlnx, so g(x)=xlnx. y1​=x2,y2​=x, W=x2(1)−x(2x)=−x2.

∫Wy2​g​dx=∫−x2x⋅xlnx​dx=−∫lnxdx=−(xlnx−x)

∫Wy1​g​dx=∫−x2x2⋅xlnx​dx=−∫xlnxdx=−(2x2​lnx−4x2​)

yp​=−x2[−(xlnx−x)]+x[−(2x2​lnx−4x2​)]=2x3​lnx−43x3​

y=C1​x2+C2​x+2x3​lnx−43x3​

Example — Technique B again

Solve x2y′′+xy′−y=x2, x>0. AE m2−1=0, roots ±1: yc​=C1​x+C2​x−1.

Transform: y¨​−y=e2t (roots ±1 match). 2 is not a root: yp​=Ae2t, 4A−A=3A=1⇒A=31​. Since e2t=x2:

y=C1​x+C2​x−1+31​x2

Comparing these examples: Technique B is preferred whenever f(x) is a pure power of x (with or without lnx); Technique A is the general-purpose fallback for any f(x).
