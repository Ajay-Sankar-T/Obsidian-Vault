Newtonian Constitutive Relation

conceptfluid-mechanicsviscous-flowconstitutive-law

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Stress in a Moving Fluid, Motion of a Fluid Element, Viscosity · formulas: Formula Bank §4

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: a Newtonian fluid has viscous stress that is a linear, isotropic function of the rate of strain: τij​=2με˙ij​+λ(∇⋅V)δij​, with Stokes' choice λ=−32​μ.

Intuition: this is the 3-D version of τ=μdu/dy. A solid's stress is proportional to its strain (Hooke's law); a fluid's viscous stress is proportional to how fast it is being strained. "Isotropic" means the fluid has no preferred direction, so only two constants can appear.

Governing Relations

τij​=2με˙ij​−32​μ(∇⋅V)δij​​,ε˙ij​=21​(∂xj​∂ui​​+∂xi​∂uj​​)

The two constants are μ (shear viscosity) and the bulk viscosity κ=λ+32​μ; Stokes' hypothesis sets κ=0.

Incompressible flow (∇⋅V=0): only μ remains, and

σxx​=−p+2μ∂x∂u​,σyy​=−p+2μ∂y∂v​,σzz​=−p+2μ∂z∂w​

τxy​=μ(∂y∂u​+∂x∂v​),τyz​=μ(∂z∂v​+∂y∂w​),τzx​=μ(∂x∂w​+∂z∂u​)

Simple shear u=u(y): τxy​=μdu/dy, Newton's law of viscosity.

Stokes' three assumptions: (1) τ is a linear function of the velocity gradients; (2) the fluid is isotropic; (3) τ=0 when the fluid is at rest or in rigid-body rotation (so only ε˙, not the rotation, enters).

Where: μ = dynamic viscosity (Pa·s); ε˙ij​ = rate of strain tensor (1/s).

Assumptions: Newtonian (constant μ at fixed T, p): water, air, oils, gases. Not for polymers, blood at low shear, paints (non-Newtonian fluids).

Visual

The visual is the decomposition already shown in Motion of a Fluid Element: of the two parts of the velocity gradient, only the strain-rate part (stretching and shearing) produces stress; the rotation part does not. The stress figure in Stress in a Moving Fluid shows the components this law fills in.

When To Use

  • Writing the Navier–Stokes equations (inserting τ in Cauchy's equation).
  • Computing wall shear stress or the viscous normal stress in a given flow.
  • Understanding which fluids it does not cover (non-Newtonian).

Recognition pattern: "Newtonian fluid", "stress proportional to rate of strain", "viscous normal stress", "stress tensor from a velocity field" → τij​=2με˙ij​.

Method

  1. Identify the velocity field and check ∇⋅V=0 (incompressible).
  2. Choose the needed stress component.
  3. Apply τij​=μ(∂j​ui​+∂i​uj​) (incompressible) and add −p on the diagonal for σij​.
  4. Check: shear stress arises from both cross derivatives; a rigid rotation gives zero stress; at a no-slip wall in unidirectional flow only μ∂u/∂n remains.

Derivation / Why It Works

Linear and isotropic means the fourth-order tensor connecting τij​ to ∂uk​/∂xl​ is built from δ's only, which leaves just two constants (λ,μ). Requiring no stress in rigid rotation removes the antisymmetric part of ∇V, so τij​=2με˙ij​+λδij​ε˙kk​. The trace of this expression gives τkk​=(2μ+3λ)∇⋅V=3κ∇⋅V, so Stokes' λ=−32​μ sets κ=0: the mechanical and thermodynamic pressures coincide. For incompressible flow this term vanishes anyway.

Common Traps

  • Trap: using only μ∂u/∂y for shear stress in a general 2-D flow.
  • Avoid it by: including both ∂u/∂y and ∂v/∂x.
  • Trap: attributing stress to vorticity: rotation does not stress a Newtonian fluid.
  • Trap: dropping the viscous normal stresses 2μ∂u/∂x at a stagnation point; they exist but are small compared with p unless μ is large.
  • Trap: applying the law to non-Newtonian fluids.

Example

Given

Water (μ=10−3 Pa·s) in the field u=3y, v=2x (s⁻¹), and in the stagnation flow u=Kx, v=−Ky with K=10 s⁻¹.

Solution

Field 1: τxy​=μ(uy​+vx​)=10−3(3+2)=5×10−3 Pa. Field 2: τxx​=2μux​=2(10−3)(10)=0.02 Pa, τyy​=−0.02 Pa, τxy​=0, so

σxx​=−p+0.02,σyy​=−p−0.02 Pa.

Answer: 0.005 Pa shear in field 1; ±0.02 Pa normal viscous stresses in the stagnation flow. Key takeaway: the viscous stresses in water are tiny compared with the pressure scale (kilopascals), which is why inviscid theory works away from walls.
