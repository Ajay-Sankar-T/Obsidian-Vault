Flow Similarity and Model Studies

conceptfluid-mechanicsdimensional-analysissimilitudemodel-testing

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Important Dimensionless Groups, Buckingham π Theorem · formulas: Formula Bank §7 · problems: Solved Problems

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: a model flow reproduces a prototype flow when it is geometrically, kinematically and dynamically similar, which in practice means every relevant dimensionless group has the same value in both.

Intuition: the Π theorem says the result Π1​=G(Π2​,…,Πk​) depends only on the groups. If you build a smaller (or cheaper) model and make Π2​,…,Πk​ equal, then Π1​ must be equal too, so you can measure it on the model and scale it up.

Governing Relations

Similarity Meaning Requirement
Geometric all lengths in the same ratio α=Lm​/Lp​, same angles, scaled roughness prerequisite
Kinematic same velocity ratio at homologous points, same streamline pattern follows from dynamic similarity
Dynamic same ratio of forces (inertia : viscous : gravity ...) at homologous points equal Re, Fr, We, Ma... as relevant

Complete similarity: Πi,m​=Πi,p​ for all independent groups i≥2, hence Π1,m​=Π1,p​.

Which groups matter (incompressible flow):

  • no free surface: equal Re;
  • free surface (ships, dams, rivers): equal Re and Fr (and We, Ca if relevant);
  • compressible: equal Re and Ma.

Scaling laws with α=Lm​/Lp​, same gravity:

Froude: Vp​Vm​​=α1/2, Qp​Qm​​=α5/2, Fp​Fm​​=ρp​ρm​​α3, Tp​Tm​​=α1/2

Reynolds: Vp​Vm​​=νp​νm​​α1​,Fp​Fm​​=ρp​νp2​ρm​νm2​​

Both Re and Fr:  νm​/νp​=α3/2 (impossible for α≪1 with ordinary fluids, see the figure in Important Dimensionless Groups).

Where: subscripts m = model, p = prototype; F = force of the same type (drag, load).

Assumptions: geometric similarity including roughness and fine details; all relevant variables included in the Π analysis.

Visual

See the scaling figure in Important Dimensionless Groups (FM-D1) and the force-scale table: Froude scaling gives V∝L1/2 and F∝L3; Reynolds scaling gives V∝1/L and F independent of L.

When To Use

  • Testing ship hulls, dams, spillways, harbours in a laboratory (Froude).
  • Wind tunnel and water tunnel tests of aircraft, cars, buildings (Reynolds and Mach).
  • Reading a published coefficient CD​(Re) and applying it at a different size or speed.

Recognition pattern: "a 1:25 model is tested at ...; find the corresponding prototype speed/force" → similarity.

Method

  1. Identify the governing groups (list the variables, form the Π groups, see Buckingham π Theorem).
  2. Choose the dominant group to match (usually Fr for free surfaces, Re otherwise); fix the length ratio.
  3. Apply the matching condition to get the model speed; use the force or flow scaling to convert measurements.
  4. Check that the neglected groups are unimportant: compressibility (Ma<0.3), surface tension (We≫1), laminar–turbulent state at model Re.

Derivation / Why It Works

Take Froude matching: Vm​/gLm​​=Vp​/gLp​​ gives Vm​/Vp​=α1/2. Forces scale as pressure times area, F∼ρV2L2, so Fm​/Fp​=(ρm​/ρp​)α⋅α2=(ρm​/ρp​)α3. Flow rate Q∼VL2 gives α5/2. For Reynolds matching, Vm​/Vp​=(νm​/νp​)/α, and F∼ρV2L2 then gives Fm​/Fp​=ρm​νm2​/(ρp​νp2​), independent of size.

Incomplete similarity

When two groups conflict (the usual case with Re and Fr), match the dominant one and correct for the other:

Situation Practice
ship, dam, harbour match Fr; Re is violated by a factor 10–1000; apply a viscous (friction) correction, use roughened models
wind tunnel for a car or aircraft match Re if Ma<0.3; otherwise a pressurised or cryogenic tunnel raises Re
sphere, cylinder drag CD​ nearly constant for 103≲Re≲2×105, so only Re in that band is needed
rivers and estuaries distorted models (vertical scale larger than horizontal) with added roughness

Common Traps

  • Trap: equating velocities or forces by the length ratio alone.
  • Why: the velocity ratio depends on which group is matched (α1/2 against 1/α).
  • Avoid it by: writing the matching equation first, then the scaling.
  • Trap: a model that "looks right" but has different ratios in different directions, or no roughness scaling.
  • Trap: matching Re in water for an air prototype and forgetting to check Ma in the original.
  • Trap: applying the force ratio with the wrong density ratio when the model fluid differs.

Example

Given

(a) A 100 m ship at 10 m/s is tested with a 1:25 model in water. (b) A car moving at 30 m/s in air (ν=1.5×10−5 m²/s, ρ=1.2 kg/m³) is tested as a 1:4 model in a water tunnel (ν=1.0×10−6, ρ=998).

Solution

(a) Froude scaling, α=1/25:

Vm​=101/25​=2 m/s,Fp​Fm​​=α3=156251​.

(b) Reynolds scaling, α=1/4:

Vm​=Vp​νp​νm​​α1​=30×1.5×10−510−6​×4=8.0 m/s,

Fp​Fm​​=ρp​νp2​ρm​νm2​​=1.2×2.25×10−10998×10−12​=3.70.

Check via F=CD​21​ρV2A: 1.2998​×(308​)2×161​=3.70. ✓.

Answer: (a) 2 m/s and a force ratio of 1/15 625; (b) 8.0 m/s and a model force 3.70 times the prototype force. Key takeaway: water is a good wind-tunnel substitute for air because its small ν lets you reach high Re at modest speed (8 m/s instead of 120 m/s, which would be Ma=0.35 in air).
