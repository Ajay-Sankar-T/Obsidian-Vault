Saint-Venant's principle is an exponential decay law — just in space

Axial Deformation mentions Saint-Venant's principle only qualitatively (stress concentrations near a load smooth out "away from" the load). What's not stated there, but is true and is the useful cross-domain fact: for many elasticity problems, the rate at which a self-equilibrated local stress disturbance decays with distance x from its source is genuinely exponential, σ(x)∼σ0​e−x/ℓ, where ℓ is a decay length set by the cross-section's own geometry (roughly the member's depth/width) — this is the direct spatial analogue of the temporal decay in First-Order AC Transients, with distance playing the role of time and cross-sectional geometry playing the role of RC or L/R. In both cases, the decay constant depends only on the system's own "storage geometry," never on the size of the initial disturbance — a 10× larger point load decays over the same distance ℓ, just from a larger σ0​, exactly as a 10× larger initial capacitor voltage decays over the same τ=RC.

Damping ratio ζ as decay-rate-relative-to-oscillation-rate

Second-Order AC Transients's ζ=α/ωn​ is a dimensionless ratio of two rates: how fast the envelope decays (α) versus how fast the system would oscillate if undamped (ωn​). This exact ratio-of-rates idea is implicit but never named in Euler Buckling of Columns's slenderness ratio KL/r: slenderness is a ratio of how far the load has to act (KL) to how resistant the cross-section is to bending (r, radius of gyration) — a geometric damping ratio for stability rather than a temporal one, and it plays the identical classifying role (ζ<1 underdamped ↔ high slenderness = Euler-buckling regime; ζ>1 overdamped ↔ low slenderness = crushing/yield regime dominates instead, per Euler Buckling of Columns's "short/stubby vs long/slender" split).

Consequence you probably didn't have before

Both your circuits vault and your structures vault teach "find τ (or ℓ, or slenderness) then classify the regime" as a procedure, but never state the reason the procedure works: any linear system's transient behavior is fully characterized by the ratio of its own internal rate constants — never by the size of the disturbance. This is why superposition (Week 7) and the elastic superposition used throughout Strength of Materials (e.g. Unsymmetric and Combined Bending) both work: linearity guarantees the decay/oscillation shape is disturbance-independent, so responses to different loads can be added and only the amplitudes combine.
