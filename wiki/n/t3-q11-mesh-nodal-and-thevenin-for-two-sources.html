T3-Q11: A Two-Source AC Circuit, Solved by Mesh, Nodal and Thevenin

EE1100tutorial-solutionAC-circuitsmesh-analysisnodal-analysis

Hub: Tutorial 3 - AC Circuit Analysis · Theory: Nodal and Mesh Analysis (AC), Thevenin, Norton and Maximum Power Transfer (AC) · Previous: T3-Q10 - Making the Source Current In Phase · Next: T3-Q12 - AC Maximum Power Transfer

For the circuit of Fig. 5 (Vˉ1​=10∠0∘, Vˉ2​=10∠−60∘ V, Z1​=1+j1, Z2​=1−j1, Z3​=1+j2 Ω): (a) find the mesh currents Iˉ1​,Iˉ2​ and the current through Z3​ by the mesh method; (b) repeat by the nodal method; (c) find the current through Z3​ using Thevenin's equivalent. [Sheet: Iˉ1​=1.58∠60∘, Iˉ2​=6.05∠94.3∘, IˉZ3​​=4.82∠−75∘ A]

Tests: three methods on one circuit: mesh, nodal, Thevenin; they must agree.

Given

Left mesh: Vˉ1​, Z1​=R1​+jX1​=1+j1, shared Z3​=R3​+jX3​=1+j2. Right mesh: Z2​=R2​−jX2​=1−j1, Vˉ2​ (+ at top), shared Z3​. Both mesh currents are drawn clockwise.

Principle and why it applies

The circuit has two meshes and two nodes (a, b) so it is a fair test of choosing a method; all three give identical IˉZ3​​ because they solve the same linear system. The phasor methods are the DC methods with R→Z (Theory - Circuit Laws and Network Theorems).

Diagram

Solution

(a) Mesh. Both currents clockwise: the shared Z3​ carries Iˉ1​−Iˉ2​ downward; Vˉ2​ is traversed from its + terminal to −, a voltage drop:

(Z1​+Z3​)Iˉ1​−Z3​Iˉ2​=Vˉ1​:(2+j3)Iˉ1​−(1+j2)Iˉ2​=10,

−Z3​Iˉ1​+(Z2​+Z3​)Iˉ2​=−Vˉ2​:−(1+j2)Iˉ1​+(2+j1)Iˉ2​=−5+j8.66.

Determinant Δ=(2+j3)(2+j1)−(1+j2)2=(1+j8)−(−3+j4)=4+j4=5.657∠45∘. Cramer's rule:

Iˉ1​=Δ10(2+j1)+(1+j2)(−5+j8.66)​=4+j4−2.32+j8.66​=1.585∠60∘ A,

Iˉ2​=Δ(2+j3)(−5+j8.66)+10(1+j2)​=4+j4−25.98+j22.32​=6.055∠94.3∘ A.

Current through Z3​ (top to bottom):

IˉZ3​​=Iˉ1​−Iˉ2​=4.83∠−75∘ A.

(b) Nodal (unknown Vˉa​, b = reference). Y1​=0.5−j0.5, Y2​=0.5+j0.5, Y3​=1+j21​=0.2−j0.4 S:

Vˉa​=Y1​+Y2​+Y3​Vˉ1​Y1​+Vˉ2​Y2​​=1.2−j0.4(5−j5)+(6.83−j1.83)​=1.2−j0.411.83−j6.83​=10.80∠−11.6∘ V,

IˉZ3​​=Z3​Vˉa​​=2.236∠63.4∘10.80∠−11.6∘​=4.83∠−75∘ A  ✓.

(c) Thevenin (remove Z3​; looking into a-b):

Zth​=Z1​∥Z2​=2(1+j1)(1−j1)​=1 Ω,Vˉth​=Z1​+Z2​Vˉ1​Z2​+Vˉ2​Z1​​=13.66∠−30∘ V

(explicitly: Vˉ1​Z2​=10−j10, Vˉ2​Z1​=(5−j8.66)(1+j)=13.66−j3.66; the sum is 23.66−j13.66 and half of it is 11.83−j6.83=13.66∠−30∘). Then

IˉZ3​​=Zth​+Z3​Vˉth​​=2+j213.66∠−30∘​=2.828∠45∘13.66∠−30∘​=4.83∠−75∘ A  ✓.

Result

Iˉ1​=1.58∠60∘, Iˉ2​=6.05∠94.3∘, IˉZ3​​=4.83∠−75∘ A (by all three methods)​

(the sheet prints 4.82; the exact magnitude is 4.830.)

Check

Three independent routes give the same 4.83∠−75∘ A, which is itself the check. Dimensionally, at node a: the current Iˉ1​ arrives from the left, Iˉ2​ is the (clockwise) current of the right mesh arriving at a from Z3​'s side, and the difference Iˉ1​−Iˉ2​ flows down through Z3​ ✓.

Trap / alternative

  • The mesh equation for mesh 2 has −Vˉ2​ on the right because the source is traversed against its polarity; mis-signing it changes the answer completely.
  • IˉZ3​​=Iˉ1​−Iˉ2​, not Iˉ1​+Iˉ2​ (both clockwise meshes pass through Z3​ in opposite directions).
  • For a circuit with a load that changes, the Thevenin route (one Zth​, Vˉth​, then divide) is the fastest; it also sets up T3-Q12 - AC Maximum Power Transfer.
