Fluids and Solids — Pressure, Stress and Constitutive Laws

synthesiscross-domainstresspressureviscosity

← up: Cross-Domain Synthesis MOC

Fluids and solids are described by the same stress tensor. What distinguishes them is only the constitutive law — what stress the material develops for a given deformation.

Pressure is the isotropic stress state

In a static fluid, shear stress is zero, so σx​=σy​=σz​=−p on every plane (Fluids and Solids). Every plane is principal and the equal principal stresses make Mohr's circle a single point (Mohr's Circle for Stress). A solid under hydrostatic compression is in the same state; a fluid just can never leave it while at rest.

The same cut gives pressure force and hoop stress

Hydrostatic Force on Curved Surfaces uses: the net force of a uniform pressure on a curved surface, in any direction, equals pressure times the projected area in that direction. Thin-walled pressure vessels is the same trick turned around: cut a cylinder of radius r, length L, wall t along its axis. The pressure pushes with p(2rL) on the projected area; the wall resists with 2σh​tL, so

σh​=tpr​,σl​=2tpr​ (end cap: pπr2=σl​2πrt).

The fluid supplies the load, the solid answers with stress; the force balance is identical.

Sources: Generalized Hooke's Law, Viscosity. The two laws have the same form with G→μ and γ→γ˙​: the dashpot response is the time derivative of the spring response, which is why a spring and a dashpot differ by one factor of jω in the frequency domain.

A new bridge: the Deborah number is the Maxwell time constant

Put an elastic spring (stiffness G) in series with a viscous dashpot (μ): the Maxwell model, the simplest viscoelastic material. This model is not in the vault yet, but it is the missing piece between the two notes above. In the force-voltage dictionary of the impedance analogy a dashpot is R and a spring is 1/C, so the Maxwell element is a series RC circuit and its time constant is

λ=Gμ​↔τ=RC.

Deborah number De=λ/tflow​ then reads as tobs​/τ from Exponential Decay, inverted: De≪1 is steady state (fluid), De≫1 is mid-transient (solid). The same material is solid or fluid depending on which side of its own time constant you look.

Common traps

  • Quoting a gauge pressure where a stress is needed: sign (compression negative) and reference differ.
  • Applying σh​=pr/t to a thick wall (r/t≥10 rule breaks).
  • Using μ where only a static solid shear exists, or G for flow.
