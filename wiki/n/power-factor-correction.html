The goal (Q10 pattern)

Find a reactance to add to a circuit so that the source current ends up in phase with the source voltage — i.e. drive the net power factor to unity.

Derivation

From Iˉ=Vˉ/Z, I and V are in phase exactly when ∠Z=0°, i.e. when

Im(Z)=0

So whatever reactance X the existing circuit presents, an equal-and-opposite reactance −X must be added in series to cancel it (or, in parallel form, an admittance chosen to cancel Im(Y)):

  • Inductive circuit (Z=R+jX, X>0): needs a capacitor in series (or parallel, depending on configuration) to contribute −jX.
  • Capacitive circuit (X<0): needs an inductor to contribute +j∣X∣.

In practice almost all correction is capacitive, because almost all real inductive loads (motors, transformers, fluorescent ballasts) present a lagging power factor that needs cancelling — this is why power factor correction is essentially synonymous with "add a capacitor bank" in industrial practice.

Sizing the capacitor

If the required capacitive reactance is XC​:

XC​=ωC1​⟹C=ωXC​1​

Worked example (Q10): existing inductive reactance is cancelled by adding X=−j47.05 Ω, giving C=53.13 μF at the circuit's operating frequency, and unity power factor at the source.

Why this matters (beyond the algebra)

Reactive power Q doesn't do useful work but still draws current through the supply, transformers, and wiring (see AC Power and Power Factor — ∣S∣=P2+Q2​ always ≥P). Utilities size and rate equipment by apparent power ∣S∣, not real power P, so a poor power factor forces oversized (and more expensive) generation, transmission, and switchgear for the same useful P delivered — the economic reason correction capacitors are installed at industrial sites with large motor loads.

Relation to conjugate matching

Power-factor correction and AC maximum power transfer both use "add a reactance to cancel another reactance," but for opposite purposes: correction cancels the load's own reactance to make the source see a purely resistive (efficient) circuit; conjugate matching chooses the load's reactance to cancel the source's internal reactance, maximizing power delivered to that load (often at the cost of efficiency, since half the power is dissipated in RTh​ itself) — don't confuse the two design goals.
