Unsymmetric and Combined Bending

Related: Flexure Formula · Axial Deformation · Plane Stress Transformation

Unsymmetric Bending (Bending About a Non-Principal Axis, or Biaxial Bending)

If a beam is bent by moments Mz​ and My​ about both centroidal axes simultaneously (e.g. a beam loaded off the plane of symmetry), and the section has centroidal principal axes y,z (products of inertia Iyz​=0), the stress at a point (y,z) superposes both effects:

σ(y,z)=−Iz​Mz​y​+Iy​My​z​

(sign conventions vary; the key idea is linear superposition of two single-axis flexure formulas, each exactly like Flexure Formula).

The neutral axis in unsymmetric bending is generally not aligned with either the load direction or a principal axis — it's the line where σ=0, found by setting the combined expression to zero. If the section's principal axes aren't known (product of inertia Iyz​=0), the general (unsymmetric) flexure formula requires the full inertia tensor; it's usually simpler to first rotate to principal axes (an eigenvalue problem, exactly analogous to the principal-stress calculation in Plane Stress Transformation).

Combined Axial Load and Bending (Eccentric Loading)

A load P applied off-center (eccentricity e from the centroid) is statically equivalent to a centroidal axial force P plus a moment M=Pe. By superposition:

σ(y)=AP​±IMy​=AP​±IPey​

This combines Axial Deformation's uniform stress with Flexure Formula's linear bending stress. The kern (core) of a section is the region within which an eccentric load produces no tensile stress anywhere on the section — important for masonry/concrete design where tension isn't permitted.

General Combined Loading

For a member simultaneously subject to axial force N, bending moments My​,Mz​, and (for a shaft) torque T, the normal stress at a point superposes the axial and bending contributions, while shear stress superposes the torsional (Torsion of Circular Shafts) and transverse-shear (Shear Stress Formula for Beams) contributions. The resulting combined stress state at a point is then analyzed using Plane Stress Transformation to find principal stresses and the maximum shear stress governing failure.
