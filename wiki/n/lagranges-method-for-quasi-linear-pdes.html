Lagrange's Method for Quasi-linear PDEs

differential-equationspdesconceptfirst-order-pdecharacteristics

Related: PDEs - MOC · Formation of PDEs - Eliminating Constants and Functions · Cauchy Problem for First Order PDEs · Complete, General and Singular Integrals · solved in Assignment 8 - First Order PDEs and Canonical Forms - Solutions Q2, Q3

What it is

For the quasi-linear equation

a(x,y,u)ux​+b(x,y,u)uy​=c(x,y,u)(Lagrange’s equation)

the PDE is turned into ODEs along curves called characteristics.

Why it works

A solution u=u(x,y) is a surface S in (x,y,u)-space with normal n=(p,q,−1). The PDE says (a,b,c)⋅n=ap+bq−c=0: the vector (a,b,c) is tangent to S at every point. So integral surfaces are exactly the surfaces tangent to the direction field (a,b,c), i.e. surfaces made of curves with direction (a,b,c):

adx​=bdy​=cdu​​(Lagrange’s auxiliary / characteristic equations)

Along any curve on S, du=pdx+qdy; compare with ap+bq=c.

Theorem. If φ(x,y,u)=c1​ and ψ(x,y,u)=c2​ are two independent solutions (first integrals) of the auxiliary equations, the general solution is G(φ,ψ)=0, G arbitrary (equivalently φ=g(ψ)). Proof sketch: dφ=0 along characteristics gives aφx​+bφy​+cφu​=0 (the same for ψ), so a:b:c equals the Jacobians ∂(y,u)∂(φ,ψ)​:∂(u,x)∂(φ,ψ)​:∂(x,y)∂(φ,ψ)​, which is exactly the PDE obtained by eliminating G (Formation of PDEs - Eliminating Constants and Functions).

Check any first integral by testing aφx​+bφy​+cφu​≡0.

Procedure

  1. Write the PDE as ap+bq=c.
  2. Write adx​=bdy​=cdu​.
  3. Find one first integral φ=c1​.
  4. Find a second, independent ψ=c2​.
  5. General solution G(φ,ψ)=0.

Finding the integrals (steps 3–4):

Technique Idea
grouping equate two ratios that involve only two variables, integrate the resulting ODE
grouping with substitution use φ=c1​ to eliminate a variable from a second pair, integrate, then put c1​=φ back
multipliers each ratio equals la+mb+ncldx+mdy+ndu​. If la+mb+nc=0 then ldx+mdy+ndu=0, and if that is exact its integral is a first integral. Choose (l,m,n)=(x,y,u), (1,1,1), (1/x,1/y,1/u), (φx​,φy​,φu​) as first tries
eigenvalue (equivalent ratio) method for linear characteristic systems v˙=Mv (a, b, c linear in x,y,u): a left eigenvector ℓ of M gives d(ℓ⋅v)=λ(ℓ⋅v)dt, so ratios such as (ℓ2​⋅v)/(ℓ1​⋅v)λ2​/λ1​ are constant. Complex eigenvalues give arctan and ln terms

Worked examples

  • ux+uy=1 is a=b=c=1: x−y=c1​, u−y=c2​, so u=y+g(x−y).
  • xp+yq=u: y/x=c1​, u/x=c2​, so u=xg(y/x) (homogeneous of degree 1).
  • x2p+y2q=(x+y)u: x1​−y1​=c1​; subtract the first two ratios, x−yd(x−y)​=udu​, so ux−y​=c2​.
  • (y−u)p+(u−x)q=x−y: multipliers (1,1,1) and (x,y,u) both give zero denominators, so x+y+u=c1​ and x2+y2+u2=c2​ (surfaces of revolution about the line x=y=u).
  • (x+y−u)p+(y+u−x)q=u+x−y: the system is v˙=Mv with M=I+N, N skew. The eigenvalues are 1 and 1±i3​, so integrals involve arctan and ln(x+y+u) (A8 Q2(y)).

Figure: left, characteristic base curves for ux​+2xuy​=y (each curve carries its own ODE for u); right, a data curve that is a characteristic (Cauchy Problem for First Order PDEs).

Traps
  • The second integral from "substitution" must have c1​ replaced by φ at the end, or it is not a function of x,y,u only.
  • Two first integrals must be independent (Jacobian of rank 2), not e.g. φ and φ2.
  • Any pair of ratios that is not independent of the third variable cannot be integrated alone: use multipliers or substitution.
