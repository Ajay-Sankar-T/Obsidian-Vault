Theory: Resonance and Passive Filters

EE1100tutorial-theoryAC-circuitsresonancefilters

← up: Tutorial 4 · lectures: Lecture 20 Resonance, Frequency Response and Filters, Lecture 21 Design of Passive Filters, Lecture 22 Design of Passive Filters (Contd…) · week: 08 Resonance and Magnetically Coupled Circuits · fuller treatments: Frequency Response and Resonance Curves, Filter Theory, Series vs Parallel RLC - Key Distinctions

The idea in one paragraph

A circuit's response to a sinusoid depends on frequency because XL​=ωL grows and XC​=1/ωC shrinks with ω. Resonance is the frequency at which the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel; the circuit then looks purely resistive, the response peaks, and the L and C voltages (or currents) are magnified by the quality factor Q. A filter is a circuit whose gain ∣H(jω)∣ passes some frequencies and attenuates others; the same RLC building blocks make low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-reject filters.

1. Frequency response and the −3 dB point

The transfer function H(jω)=Vˉout​/Vˉin​ is a complex number at each ω; its magnitude is the gain and its angle the phase shift. Gain in decibels: ∣H∣dB​=20log10​∣H∣.

The half-power (cut-off) frequencies are where ∣H∣=∣H∣max​/2​, i.e. the power has fallen to half. The bandwidth is the width of the band between them (band-pass) or the band above/below one of them (low/high-pass).

Meaning of Q: energy stored over energy lost per radian; also the voltage magnification. The half-power frequencies satisfy ∣X∣=R:

For Q≳5 the narrow-band approximation ω1,2​≈ω0​∓BW/2 is good; for small Q (Tutorial 4 Q1, Q=1) it is poor, use the exact form. The response curve is not symmetric on a linear axis (it is symmetric on a log axis, since ω1​ω2​=ω02​).

3. Parallel RLC resonance (current source)

Y(jω)=R1​+j(ωC−ωL1​),ω0​=LC​1​  (same),

At ω0​: Y=1/R (minimum), so the impedance and the voltage V=IR are maximum; the inductor and capacitor currents are equal and opposite, each QI, circulating inside the LC loop:

Q=ω0​LR​=ω0​RC=RLC​​,BW=Qω0​​=RC1​,ω1,2​=ω0​[1+4Q21​​∓2Q1​].

This is the dual of the series circuit: swap V↔I, R↔1/R, L↔C. A small parallel resistance means a low Q (heavy damping), opposite to the series case where small R means high Q.

First-order filters have a gain of 1/2​ (−3 dB) at ωc​ and roll off at 20 dB per decade. Design is inverting those formulas: choose one component, compute the other from fc​=1/(2πRC) (Q4, Q5), or from ω02​=1/LC (Q7).

5. Selectivity: how large must Q be?

For the series band-pass with x=ω/ω0​,

∣H∣=1+Q2(x−1/x)2​1​.

To attenuate a signal at relative offset x by A dB you need Q2(x−1/x)2≥10A/10−1. For −20 dB: Q∣x−1/x∣≥99​=9.95 (Q8). A frequency offset that is equal above and below ω0​ is not equally easy to reject: ∣x−1/x∣ is 0.0396 at x=1.02 but 0.0404 at x=0.98, so the high side is the tighter requirement (it needs Q≥251.2 against 246.2 for the low side) and it sets the design.

6. Practical limits

Real inductors have series resistance, real capacitors leakage and parasitics. At RF the required L and C become comparable to stray values (Q8 needs C=0.063 pF), and lumped designs give way to distributed elements. High Q means large internal voltages or currents (Q3: 500 V inside a 10 V circuit).

7. Common traps

  • Using ω0​∓BW/2 for the band edges at low Q (Q1, Q6).
  • Using the series formulas (Q=ω0​L/R, BW=R/L) for a parallel circuit.
  • Mixing ω (rad/s) and f (Hz): BW and Q are defined with the same variable; ω0​=2πf0​.
  • Forgetting that the peak voltage in a source description is not the rms (Q1(e), Q2(f)).
