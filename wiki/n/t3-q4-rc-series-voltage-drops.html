T3-Q4: Voltage Drops in an RC Series Circuit from the Phase Angle

EE1100tutorial-solutionAC-circuitsimpedanceRC-circuit

Hub: Tutorial 3 - AC Circuit Analysis · Theory: Impedance and Series-Parallel AC Circuits, Phasors and Complex Algebra · Previous: T3-Q3 - RL Series Circuit Impedance · Next: T3-Q5 - RC Circuit Power and Power Factor

An RC series circuit is driven by 170sinωt V and the steady-state current leads the voltage by 30∘. Find the effective voltage drops across the resistive and reactive elements. [Sheet: VˉR​=104.103∠30∘ V, VˉXC​​=60.14∠−60∘ V]

Tests: phasor voltage triangle; VR​ in phase with I, VC​ lagging I by 90∘.

Given

e(t)=170sinωt (peak 170 V, so rms V=170/2​=120.2 V), I leads V by 30∘. Neither R, C nor ω is given: the angle alone fixes the split.

Principle and why it applies

In a series circuit the same current flows in both elements. VR​ is in phase with I, VC​ lags I by 90∘, and KVL says the phasor sum is the source: Vˉ=VˉR​+VˉC​. So VˉR​ and VˉC​ are the two legs of a right triangle with hypotenuse Vˉ, and the angle between V and I (which is −30∘ relative to I) fixes both legs.

Diagram

Solution

Take Vˉ=120.2∠0∘ as reference, so Iˉ is at +30∘.

VR​=Vcos30∘=120.2×0.866=104.1 V,VˉR​=104.1∠+30∘ V (same angle as I),

VC​=Vsin30∘=120.2×0.5=60.1 V,VˉC​=60.1∠(30∘−90∘)=60.1∠−60∘ V.

Result

VˉR​=104.1∠30∘ V,VˉXC​​=60.1∠−60∘ V​

(effective values; the sheet prints 60.14 V, exactly 60.10 V.)

Check

Phasor sum in rectangular form: 104.1∠30∘=90.15+j52.05 and 60.1∠−60∘=30.05−j52.05; the sum is 120.2+j0 ✓ =Vˉ. The magnitudes obey V2=VR2​+VC2​: 104.12+60.12=14450=120.22 ✓. Also XC​/R=tan30∘=0.577 and pf=cos30∘=0.866 leading.

Trap / alternative

  • Effective means rms: divide the 170 V peak by 2​ before splitting.
  • The scalar sum 104.1+60.1=164.2 V does not equal the source; voltages in R and C add as phasors, 90° apart.
  • If the current lagged by 30∘ the element would be inductive and VˉL​ would be at +120∘.

Concepts used

  • Series impedance, phasor diagram, rms (Impedance and Series-Parallel AC Circuits, Phasors and Complex Algebra)
