Rotating Vectors — Phasors, Velocity Polygons and Complex Kinematics

A phasor diagram and a velocity polygon are the same construction: a closed polygon of vectors, each side a rotated and scaled copy of one underlying quantity. The link between them is one fact: differentiating a rotating vector multiplies it by jω.

Loop closure ≙ KVL

A four-bar loop obeys r1​+r2​+r3​+r4​=0; differentiating gives the velocity polygon, which also closes. A series circuit obeys ∑V=0 as phasors; the phasor diagram of VR​+VL​+VC​=Vs​ is a closed polygon whose sides are all the same current turned by 0∘, +90∘, −90∘ and scaled by ∣Z∣ of each element. In the velocity polygon each side is a different link vector turned 90∘ and scaled by that link's own ωi​. Same closure, different bookkeeping:

Phasor diagram:   one I   × (R, jωL, 1/jωC)       → sides of the voltage polygon
Velocity polygon: r_i e^{jθ_i} × (jω_i)            → sides of the velocity polygon

See Graphical Methods (Polygons) and Phasors and Complex Algebra.

A new angle: the acceleration image has an impedance angle

From eq. 2.17 the relative acceleration of two points on a rigid link has components ABω2 (along BA) and ABα (perpendicular). So the acceleration vector sits at

ψ=arctanω2α​

from the link, with magnitude ABω4+α2​. That is exactly ∣Z∣=R2+X2​ and θZ​=arctan(X/R) with ω2↔R and α↔X. The velocity image is the special case ψ=90∘ with no in-phase part. Both are rank-1 vector rotations, which is why the angle appears at 1×, not 2×; see Eigenvalues, Rotation and Diagonalization Everywhere.

Where the analogy stops

  • A phasor drops the common factor ejωt because every quantity rotates at one frequency in a linear circuit. In a mechanism each link has its own ωi​ and the geometry is nonlinear, so the polygon is valid only at one instant.
  • Coriolis acceleration 2ω×VP/i​ (sliding joints, Problem 2.19) has no phasor counterpart.
