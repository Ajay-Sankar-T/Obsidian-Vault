Ordinary and Singular Points

Related: Series Solutions - MOC · Standard Form and Homogeneity (2nd Order DE)

Standard Form

General second order linear homogeneous ODE:

a0​(x)y′′+a1​(x)y′+a2​(x)y=0.

Dividing by a0​(x), the standard form is

y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0,P(x)=a0​(x)a1​(x)​,Q(x)=a0​(x)a2​(x)​.

The nature of the solution near x=x0​ depends on the behaviour of P(x),Q(x) there — this motivates classifying x0​ as ordinary or singular.

A point x0​ is an analytic point of f if f has a Taylor series expansion around x0​.

Ordinary Point

x=x0​ is an ordinary point of y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0 if both P(x) and Q(x) are analytic at x0​ — i.e. expandable in a power series about x0​ with nonzero radius of convergence (equivalently, no singularities/poles at x0​).

This requires a0​(x0​)=0. Example: in y′′+xy′+y=0, every point is ordinary.

At an ordinary point, seek a solution as an ordinary power series:

y(x)=n=0∑∞​an​(x−x0​)n.

Singular Point

A point x0​ is singular if it is not ordinary — at least one of P(x),Q(x) fails to be analytic there. Singular points are further classified:

  • Regular singular point: P(x),Q(x) are not analytic at x0​, but (x−x0​)P(x) and (x−x0​)2Q(x) are both analytic at x0​.
  • Irregular singular point: otherwise.

Example: x2y′′+xy′+(x2−n2)y=0 (Bessel's equation) has a regular singular point at x=0. Regular singular points are treated using the Frobenius method — see Regular and Irregular Singular Points and Frobenius Theorem.

Existence Theorem

Theorem. If x0​ is an ordinary point of y′′+P(x)y′+Q(x)y=0, the general solution can be written as y=∑n=0∞​an​(x−x0​)n. This series converges at least for ∣x−x0​∣<R, where R is the distance from x0​ to the nearest singular point.

The two arbitrary constants a0​,a1​ correspond to two linearly independent solutions (recall Linear Independence and the Wronskian). For simplicity, examples typically take the ordinary point x0​=0.
