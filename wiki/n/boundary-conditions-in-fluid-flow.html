Boundary Conditions in Fluid Flow

conceptfluid-mechanicsviscous-flowboundary-conditions

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Navier-Stokes Equations · next: Plane Couette Flow

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: a boundary condition states what the velocity (and stress) must be on the edge of the flow region; the differential equation alone cannot choose among its many solutions, the boundary conditions do.

Intuition: the Navier–Stokes ODE for a unidirectional flow, μu′′=dp/dx, has a two-parameter family of solutions. Two conditions (one at each edge) fix them. Get the conditions wrong and the answer is wrong even if the algebra is perfect.

Governing Relations

Boundary Condition Statement
Solid wall no-slip fluid velocity equals wall velocity: V=Vwall​ (both tangential and normal)
Solid wall, impermeable no-penetration V⋅n=Vwall​⋅n (all that survives for an inviscid fluid)
Free surface (liquid–gas) zero shear μ∂u/∂n=0 (gas viscosity negligible)
Free surface normal stress p=patm​−σ(1/R1​+1/R2​) (surface tension, see Surface Tension)
Fluid–fluid interface velocity continuous V1​=V2​
Fluid–fluid interface shear stress continuous μ1​∂u1​/∂n=μ2​∂u2​/∂n
Symmetry plane / axis no flux, no shear Vn​=0, ∂Vt​/∂n=0; on a pipe axis: ∂u/∂r=0 (finite u)
Inlet / outlet specified V or p profile, or pressure; periodic and far-field conditions for repeating or unbounded flows

Slip at small scales: at Knudsen number Kn≳0.01 the no-slip condition fails and slip appears (Knudsen Number).

Counting rule: one condition per derivative order per direction: a second-order ODE (such as μu′′=dp/dx) needs two.

Where: n = unit normal to the boundary; σ = surface tension.

Assumptions: continuum; Newtonian fluid; the no-slip condition is an empirical fact, confirmed for ordinary liquids and gases at normal scales.

Visual

Sketch the flow region and mark each boundary with its condition. For film flow down an incline: no-slip u=0 at the bed, zero shear du/dy=0 at the free surface; for a pipe: u=0 at r=R and du/dr=0 at r=0.

When To Use

  • Whenever you integrate a viscous flow ODE (Couette, Poiseuille, film, annulus).
  • Setting up numerical simulations.
  • Interface problems (two immiscible liquids, droplets).

Recognition pattern: "wall moves at", "free surface", "two layers of different viscosity", "centreline" → choose the boundary condition for each edge.

Method

  1. Identify every boundary and what it is physically (wall, free surface, interface, axis, inlet).
  2. Choose the matching condition from the table.
  3. Apply: count conditions against the order of the equation; two for a second-order ODE.
  4. Check: the solution satisfies each condition, and signs (moving-wall direction) are consistent.

Derivation / Why It Works

No-slip: molecules adjacent to a wall exchange momentum with it frequently enough to share its velocity. Free surface: for air against water the viscosity is about 50 times smaller and the density roughly 1000 times smaller, so the gas cannot support a shear stress comparable to the liquid's: τliquid​≈0. Interface: a thin interface has no mass to accelerate, so the forces on it must balance: shear stress continuous, velocity continuous.

Common Traps

  • Trap: using no-slip on a free surface.
  • Avoid it by: zero shear there, du/dy=0, not u=0.
  • Trap: requiring u=0 on the centreline of a pipe.
  • Avoid it by: symmetry: du/dr=0 and finite velocity.
  • Trap: a wrong sign for a moving wall (direction relative to the axis).
  • Trap: neglecting surface tension at very small scales, or using slip at ordinary scales.

Example

Given

Two immiscible layers between a fixed lower plate and an upper plate moving at U=1 m/s: lower layer μ1​=0.1 Pa·s, h1​=1 mm; upper layer μ2​=0.01 Pa·s, h2​=1 mm. Find the interface velocity and the shear stress.

Approach

Each layer is a Couette flow with linear profile. Boundary conditions: u=0 at the lower plate, u=U at the upper plate, and at the interface u1​=u2​=ui​ with continuous shear.

Solution

Shear continuity: μ1​h1​ui​​=μ2​h2​U−ui​​, so

ui​=μ1​h2​+μ2​h1​μ2​h1​U​=0.1+0.010.01×1​=0.0909 m/s,

τ=μ1​h1​ui​​=0.1×0.0010.0909​=9.09 Pa.

Answer: ui​=0.091 m/s and τ=9.09 Pa throughout. Key takeaway: the interface stays close to the speed of the plate that borders the more viscous layer (ui​≈0 here); the low-viscosity layer takes almost all of the velocity change, like the large resistor in a series circuit taking most of the voltage.

Quick Recall

In one line: no-slip at walls, zero shear at free surfaces, continuous velocity and shear at interfaces, symmetry at axes.

Formula / trigger:

V=Vwall​ (wall),μ∂n​u=0 (free surface),∂n​u=0 (symmetry)

Remember: two conditions for a second-order ODE; u=0 is wrong on a free surface or centreline.
