Ideal Fluid

fluid-mechanicsfundamentals

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Viscosity and Newton's Law of Viscosity

An ideal fluid is defined as a fluid with zero viscosity (μ=0) — a hypothetical model, since every real fluid has μ>0. It is the fluid-mechanics analogue of a "rigid body" or "massless spring" in mechanics: not physically real, but a useful limiting idealization that strips away one complicating effect (viscous shear resistance) so that other phenomena (pressure forces, inertia, potential flow) can be analyzed cleanly.

Where the idealization is used

An ideal (inviscid) fluid cannot sustain any shear stress even while flowing (not just at rest, unlike a real fluid governed by Newton's Law of Viscosity). This dramatically simplifies the momentum equations — the Linear Momentum Equation's control-volume analysis, and the differential Navier-Stokes equations more generally, reduce to the (still nonlinear, but shear-free) Euler equations, the starting point for potential-flow theory.

When the idealization is reasonable

Away from solid boundaries and in regions of the flow where velocity gradients are small, viscous shear stresses (τ=μdu/dy) are often negligible compared to pressure and inertial forces — this is the basis of boundary-layer theory (a topic listed in the course outline of Introduction but not yet detailed in this vault): treat the bulk flow as ideal, and confine all viscous effects to a thin layer near solid surfaces.
