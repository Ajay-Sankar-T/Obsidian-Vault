Theory: The Three-Phase Induction Machine

EE1100tutorial-theorymachinesinduction-machine

← up: Tutorial 8 · lectures: Lecture 40 Electric Machines, Lecture 41 Induction Machine-Circuit Representation · week: 11 Three Phase Induction Machines · focused notes: Revolving Magnetic Field and Synchronous Speed (full derivation), Induction Motor - Slip, Rotor Circuit and Power Flow, Induction Machines - Types, Construction and Applications · transformer analogue: 10 Single Phase Transformers, Transformer Voltage Regulation and Efficiency

The idea in one paragraph

A three-phase stator winding produces a magnetic field of constant strength that rotates at the synchronous speed ns​=120f/P. The field sweeps across the rotor conductors, inducing currents in the short-circuited rotor (transformer action); those currents, in the same field, feel a force (the Lorentz force) that drags the rotor in the direction of the field (Lenz's law: the rotor chases the change that induces its current). The rotor can never quite catch the field, because at synchronous speed there would be no relative motion, no induced emf, no current and no torque. The difference is the slip.

1. The rotating field

Three coils 120∘ apart in space carrying currents 120∘ apart in time produce the resultant MMF

FR​(α,t)=23​Fm​sin(ωt−α),

a wave of constant amplitude that travels round the air gap. The proof (Q2) adds the three space-distributed pulsating MMFs; the "backward" terms cancel because they are three phasors 120∘ apart.

For a machine with P poles supplied at f Hz,

ns​=P120f​ rpm,ωs​=P4πf​ rad/s.

Swapping any two supply leads reverses the sequence and the direction of rotation.

2. Slip and rotor quantities

s=ns​ns​−nr​​,nr​=(1−s)ns​,f2​=sf1​ (rotor frequency),E2s​=sE20​,X2s​=sX20​.

The rotor current for a rotor with resistance R2​ and standstill reactance X20​ is

I2​=R22​+(sX20​)2​sE20​​,cosϕ2​=R22​+(sX20​)2​R2​​,

so the rotor is strongly inductive at standstill (low pf, large current) and nearly resistive at small slip (pf ≈1).

3. Equivalent circuit (per phase)

The machine is a transformer with a rotating secondary. Refer the rotor to the stator side with the effective turns ratio a=N1​/N2​: E2′​=aE2​, r2′​=a2r2​, x2′​=a2x2​, I2′​=I2​/a. At slip s the rotor branch is the standstill reactance x2′​ in series with the resistance r2′​/s:

sr2′​​=r2′​+represents the shaft powerr2′​s1−s​​​.

The exact circuit has r1​+jx1​, the shunt magnetising branch jxφ​ (in parallel with a core-loss resistance Rc​ when specified), then x2′​ and r2′​/s. The approximate circuit moves the shunt branch to the input terminals (valid because xφ​≫r1​+jx1​), so I1​=I2′​+I0​ with I0​=V1​/(Rc​∥jxφ​). The parameters come from a no-load test (I0​, core loss; slip ≈0) and a blocked-rotor test (s=1, series r1​+r2′​, x1​+x2′​), exactly as for the transformer OC/SC tests.

4. Power flow and efficiency

Pin​=3​VL​IL​cosϕ,Pg​=Pin​−Pcu1​−Pcore​,Pcu2​=sPg​,Pmech​=(1−s)Pg​,Pout​=Pmech​−PF&W​.

Pg​:Pcu2​:Pmech​=1:s:(1−s).

Torque: Tdev​=ωs​Pg​​=ωr​Pmech​​ (the same number, since ωr​=(1−s)ωs​); shaft torque Tsh​=Pout​/ωr​;  η=Pout​/Pin​. Because Pcu2​=sPg​, a motor at slip s can never be more than (1−s) efficient in the rotor stage: speed regulation and efficiency trade off.

5. The torque–slip characteristic

Replace the stator side by its Thevenin equivalent seen from the rotor branch:

Vth​=V1​r1​+j(x1​+xφ​)jxφ​​,Zth​=r1​+j(x1​+xφ​)jxφ​(r1​+jx1​)​=Rth​+jXth​.

Then I2′​=Vth​/[(Rth​+r2′​/s)+j(Xth​+x2′​)] and

T(s)=ωs​3​(Rth​+r2′​/s)2+(Xth​+x2′​)2Vth2​r2′​/s​​

(approximately, with r1​+jx1​ in series and xφ​ at the input: Zth​→r1​+jx1​, Vth​→V1​). Maximising T over r2′​/s is the maximum-power-transfer condition of Theory - Circuit Laws and Network Theorems §9:

sm​r2′​​=Zk​≡Rth2​+(Xth​+x2′​)2​,sm​=Zk​r2′​​,Tmax​=2ωs​3​Rth​+Zk​Vth2​​.

Consequences:

  • Tmax​ does not depend on the rotor resistance; sm​∝r2′​. Adding external rotor resistance (wound rotor) slides the peak towards low speed and raises the starting torque, up to Tstart​=Tmax​ when r2′​+Rext′​=Zk​.
  • T∝V2: a 10 % voltage drop costs 19 % of the torque.
  • Stable region: for 0<s<sm​ torque rises with slip, so a load increase slows the rotor and the motor produces more torque. Beyond sm​ torque falls with slip, so a load above Tmax​ stalls the motor.
  • At small slip r2′​/s≫ everything else, so T≈ωs​r2′​3V2​s: a straight line, the "constant speed" region.

6. Starting

At standstill s=1, the rotor looks like a short circuit: the starting current is 5–7 times the rated current with a poor power factor, and the starting torque is moderate. Remedies: reduced-voltage or star-delta starters for cage motors, external rotor resistance for wound-rotor motors (Induction Machines - Types, Construction and Applications).

7. Common traps

  • ns​=120f/P uses poles, not pole pairs.
  • Slip is relative to the synchronous speed; f2​=sf1​.
  • Torque is Pg​/ωs​ (or Pmech​/ωr​), never Pg​/ωr​.
  • r2′​/s is not r2′​: the extra r2′​(1−s)/s is the mechanical load.
  • Referred quantities carry a2 for impedances but a for voltages and 1/a for currents.
  • Output torque (shaft) differs from developed torque by the friction and windage loss.

8. Cross-domain links

  • Gears, Transformers and Reflected Impedance: the equivalent circuit is a transformer plus a gear: r2′​(1−s)/s is the reflected mechanical load; a high-slip (low-speed) load looks like a smaller resistance.
  • Rotating Vectors - Phasors, Velocity Polygons and Complex Kinematics and Tutorial 4 - Resonance, Filter and Three-Phase Circuits: the rotating field is three phasors 120∘ apart made physical.
  • Thevenin, Norton and Maximum Power Transfer (AC): pull-out torque is the maximum-power-transfer point of the rotor resistance.
