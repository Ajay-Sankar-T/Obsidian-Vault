Stability of Floating Bodies — Metacentre

Why floating bodies need a different stability criterion than submerged ones

For a fully submerged body (Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle), the centre of buoyancy B stays fixed relative to the body no matter how it's oriented (the whole body displaces fluid, always the same shape of displaced volume). But for a floating body, tilting changes which part of the body is underwater, so the displaced volume's shape — and hence B — shifts with every tilt. "Is B above G" is no longer a fixed fact about the body; it must be re-examined after a small tilt.

The metacentre

When a floating body tilts through a small angle θ, the centre of buoyancy moves, B→B′. The vertical line of action of the new buoyant force (through B′) intersects the original vertical line (through B) at a point M, the metacentre. For small tilts, M's position is essentially independent of θ — it depends only on the body's geometry.

Metacentric radius

BM=Vsub​IO​​​

IO​ — second moment of the waterplane area (see Second Moments of Area) about the tilt axis; Vsub​ — submerged volume. Also, from the small-angle geometry of the shifted buoyancy line:

BM=tanθxˉ​​⟹tanθxˉ​=BM=Vsub​IO​​

Metacentric height — the master stability formula

From the geometry BM=BG+GM:

GM=BM−BG​⟹GM=Vsub​IO​​−BG​

This is the formula to use directly in numerical stability problems.

Stability criterion

GM Meaning Stability
GM>0 M above G stable
GM<0 G above M unstable
GM=0 M=G neutral

Restoring moment

For a small tilt θ:

MR​=W(GM)sinθ​ ≈ W(GM)θ​(sinθ≈θ for small θ)

GM<0 is a buckling failure">

Cross-domain: metacentric height is a stiffness, and GM<0 is a buckling failure

MR​≈W(GM)θ is a restoring-moment-proportional-to-angle law — a rotational spring with "stiffness" W⋅GM. This has the exact same mathematical shape as The Universal Second-Order Linear System's restoring force in a spring-mass system, and the stability boundary GM=0 is precisely the same kind of eigenvalue crossing as P=Pcr​ in Euler Buckling of Columns: below the critical value the small-perturbation restoring "stiffness" is positive (stable), and at/above it, the sign flips and any small disturbance grows instead of decaying. A ship capsizing when overloaded or top-heavy (GM driven negative by raising G) is the fluid-mechanics twin of a column buckling when it's too slender or too heavily loaded (EI too small or P too large relative to L) — both are "the restoring generalized-stiffness term in a linearized equilibrium equation has changed sign." See Resonance and Buckling — Instability as an Eigenvalue Problem for the general pattern this belongs to.
