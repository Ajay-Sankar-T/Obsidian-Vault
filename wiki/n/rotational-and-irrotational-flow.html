Rotational and Irrotational Flow

conceptfluid-mechanicskinematicsirrotationalpotential-flow

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Vorticity and Circulation, Differential Continuity Equation · formulas: Formula Bank §6

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: a flow is irrotational where ∇×V=0 (fluid elements do not spin) and rotational otherwise; irrotational flow can be described by a single scalar potential ϕ with V=∇ϕ.

Intuition: an irrotational flow is the smooth, "no shear, no swirl" part of the motion, typically the region outside boundary layers. It is mathematically far easier because three velocity components collapse into one scalar function.

Governing Relations

Irrotational:  ∇×V=0 ⟺ V=∇ϕ,u=ϕx​, v=ϕy​, w=ϕz​

Incompressible and irrotational: Laplace's equation

∇2ϕ=0​,plane:  u=ϕx​=ψy​,  v=ϕy​=−ψx​,  ∇2ψ=0

  • Streamlines (ψ= const) and potential lines (ϕ= const) are orthogonal except at stagnation points.
  • In irrotational flow the Bernoulli constant is the same everywhere, not only along one streamline (Bernoulli Equation).
  • Rotational plane flow has ∇2ψ=−ζz​=0.

Assumptions: simply connected region for a single-valued ϕ (the free vortex has a multi-valued ϕ=Kθ).

Visual

The same two motion figures that appear in Motion of a Fluid Element decide the question: if the element only stretches and shears but its axes show no net rotation (stagnation flow, the dashed-to-filled picture in FM-K4), the flow is irrotational.

Figure: an element in the stagnation flow V=xi−yj is stretched in x and squeezed in y by factors e±t; it stays rectangular (irrotational) and its area is conserved (incompressible).

When To Use

  • Whenever the flow is a steady, high-Re region away from walls: use potential flow (Laplace) for pressures on bodies.
  • To justify applying Bernoulli between different streamlines.
  • To construct flows by superposition (uniform stream + source + vortex).

Recognition pattern: "outside the boundary layer", "ideal flow past a cylinder", "potential function" → irrotational flow.

Method

  1. Identify the velocity field; check continuity first.
  2. Choose the test: compute ζ=∇×V.
  3. Apply: if ζ=0, integrate u=ϕx​ in x, add f(y), then match v=ϕy​ to fix f; likewise for ψ.
  4. Check: ∇2ϕ=0, orthogonality of streamlines and potential lines, and the sign convention of ψ.

Derivation / Why It Works

The curl of a gradient is identically zero, so any V=∇ϕ is irrotational. Conversely, if the curl vanishes everywhere in a simply connected region, the line integral ∮V⋅dl=0 (Stokes) so ϕ=∫V⋅dl is path-independent. Substituting V=∇ϕ into ∇⋅V=0 gives ∇2ϕ=0.

Common Traps

  • Trap: deciding rotationality from the streamline shape.
  • Why: straight streamlines in shear flow are rotational; curved streamlines of a free vortex are irrotational.
  • Avoid it by: computing the curl.
  • Trap: using Bernoulli between two streamlines in a rotational flow.
  • Trap: forgetting the singularity at a vortex axis.
  • Trap: mixing the sign conventions of ψ between textbooks.

Example

Given

The flow u=x2−y2, v=−2xy (from the continuity example). Is it irrotational? Find ϕ.

Solution

ζz​=vx​−uy​=−2y−(−2y)=0  ✓,∇⋅V=2x−2x=0  ✓

Integrate u=ϕx​: ϕ=x3/3−xy2+f(y); v=ϕy​=−2xy+f′(y)=−2xy⇒f= const.

ϕ=3x3​−xy2​,ψ=x2y−3y3​.

Answer: irrotational and incompressible, with ϕ=x3/3−xy2. Key takeaway: passing both tests means the flow can be described by ϕ, and Bernoulli's constant is global.
