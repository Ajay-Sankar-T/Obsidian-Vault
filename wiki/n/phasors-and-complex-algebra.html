Phasors and Complex Algebra

EE1100AC-circuitsphasorscomplex-numbers

← up: Tutorial 3 · builds on: Week 5, RMS and Average Value

Why phasors exist

In the time domain, RLC element equations are differential (vL​=Ldi/dt, iC​=Cdv/dt) — solving a circuit means solving simultaneous ODEs. A phasor freezes a sinusoid's amplitude and phase into a single complex number, turning differentiation/integration into multiplication/division by jω, and turning circuit analysis into ordinary complex-number algebra:

Vˉ=IˉZ

exactly like Ohm's law — see Impedance and Series-Parallel AC Circuits for why Z absorbs the jω entirely, leaving this one algebraic relation for R, L, and C alike.

Definition

v(t)=Vm​sin(ωt+θ)⟶Vˉ=2​Vm​​∠θ

(RMS magnitude — see RMS and Average Value — with ωt dropped since it is common to every quantity in a fixed-frequency circuit).

Polar ↔ rectangular conversion

Polar → rectangular: A∠θ=Acosθ+jAsinθ=x+jy.

Rectangular → polar: for z=x+jy, magnitude ∣z∣=x2+y2​, angle θ=tan−1(y/x) — always check the quadrant of (x,y) before trusting the calculator's principal value of tan−1; it silently drops 180° when x<0.

The imaginary unit j

j=−1​,j2=−1,j1​=−j,j=ej90∘

Powers cycle with period 4: j0=1, j1=j, j2=−1, j3=−j, j4=1,… — this cycling is exactly why multiplying by j is a 90° rotation, the geometric fact underlying every "L leads by 90°" / "C lags by 90°" statement.

Phasor addition — vectors, not scalars

Phasors add as vectors, not as magnitudes:

wrong: I=I1​+I2​+I3​ (magnitudes)correct: Iˉ=Iˉ1​+Iˉ2​+Iˉ3​ (complex numbers)

Procedure: convert every phasor to rectangular form, add real parts and imaginary parts separately, convert the sum back to polar. This is the mechanism behind combining parallel branch currents (KCL, see Nodal and Mesh Analysis (AC)) and series element voltages (Vˉ=VˉR​+VˉL​+VˉC​, see Impedance and Series-Parallel AC Circuits).

Worked pattern (Q6-style): given i(t)=Im​sin(ωt+θ), convert to Iˉ=2​Im​​∠θ; if a branch current is given as a cosine, first use cosθ=sin(θ+90°) (see RMS note) before writing the phasor, then add rectangular components.

Division of phasors (AC Ohm's law, polar shortcut)

If V=V∠θV​ and Z=Z∠θZ​:

I=ZV​=∣Z∣∣V∣​∠(θV​−θZ​)⟹θI​=θV​−θZ​

This single relation answers most "does current lead or lag" questions directly, without separately reasoning about each element — the sign of θZ​ (from Z=R+jX) tells you everything: θZ​>0 (inductive) means I lags V; θZ​<0 (capacitive) means I leads V.

Converting back to the time domain

If Xˉ=X∠θ is an RMS phasor, the corresponding time-domain signal is

x(t)=2​Xsin(ωt+θ)
