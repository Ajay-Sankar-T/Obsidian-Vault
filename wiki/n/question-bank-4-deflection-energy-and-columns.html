Twelve conceptual questions and ten numerical problems (Q1–Q10): standard deflections, moment-area, an overhang, a propped cantilever, Macaulay with two loads, a Castigliano frame, impact, Euler and Johnson columns and the secant formula. Part A — Conceptual questions

A1. Which boundary conditions does the double-integration method need, and how many constants are there?

Two constants per segment. Pinned or roller support: v=0. Fixed end: v=0 and θ=0. Free end: M=0 and V=0 (no condition on v). At a junction between segments, v and θ are continuous. Macaulay's method removes the junction conditions and leaves only the two end conditions.

A2. What is the rule for Macaulay's brackets?

Write M using terms ⟨x−a⟩n that are zero for x<a. Integrate each term as a whole (the bracket is carried through the integration). A UDL that stops before the end is extended to the end and cancelled by an opposite UDL starting at the stopping point. One equation then covers the whole beam.

A3. State the two moment-area theorems.

(1) The change in slope between A and B equals the area of the M/EI diagram between them. (2) The vertical deviation of B from the tangent at A equals the moment of that area about B. Positive M makes the beam sag, and the areas are signed.

A4. When can deflections be superposed?

When the material is linear elastic and the deflections are small, so that the equations are linear in the loads and the geometry does not change appreciably. It fails for large deflections, for buckling (the axial load changes the stiffness) and for materials that yield.

A5. State Maxwell's reciprocal theorem and one use.

The deflection at A due to a unit load at B equals the deflection at B due to a unit load at A (δAB​=δBA​). Use: measure the deflection curve of a beam by moving a dial gauge instead of the load (the three-point-bending lab moves the load to L/4 and 3L/4 and the readings reciprocate).

A6. Why can't strain energies of two loads simply be added?

U is quadratic in the loads, so U(P1​+P2​)=U1​+U2​+cross term. Only for loads that do no work on each other's deflections (e.g. axial and bending in a straight beam, or loads in orthogonal planes) does it separate. For a single load, U=21​Pδ gives δ directly but only at the load point and in the load direction.

A7. How does Castigliano's second theorem give a deflection at a point where there is no load?

Place a fictitious (dummy) load Q at that point and in the wanted direction, write M (and T, N) including Q, differentiate U with respect to Q, and then set Q=0. Since ∂M/∂Q is needed before Q is set to zero, only the derivative is carried, which is the moment due to a unit load there.

A8. What is the impact factor?

n=δmax​/δst​, the ratio of the dynamic to the static deflection (and stress) for the same weight. A weight dropped from h gives n=1+1+2h/δst​​; a suddenly applied load (h=0) gives n=2. A softer structure (large δst​) has a smaller n, which is why shock absorbers are compliant.

A9. List the assumptions of Euler's column theory.

Perfectly straight, homogeneous and linearly elastic column; load exactly axial and through the centroid; ideal end conditions; small deflections (1/ρ≈v′′); no residual stress or initial crookedness; and σcr​ below the proportional limit. Real columns fail below the Euler load because none of these holds exactly.

A10. What is effective length, and why is K=0.5 for a fixed–fixed column?

Le​=KL is the length of the equivalent pin-ended column, i.e. the distance between inflection points of the buckled shape. A fixed–fixed column buckles in a full sine wave with two inflection points at L/4 and 3L/4, so the middle half behaves like a pin-ended column of length L/2.

A11. Why does Euler's formula fail for short columns, and what replaces it?

Euler gives σcr​=π2E/λ2, which exceeds the yield stress for small λ, which is impossible. For λ<λc​=π2E/σY​​ the column yields before it buckles elastically, and a parabola (Johnson) or a straight line joins the yield cut-off to the Euler curve at σY​/2.

A12. Will using a higher-strength steel raise the buckling load of a slender column?

No. Pcr​=π2EI/(KL)2 contains E and I only, and all steels have E≈200 GPa. Strength matters only for short and intermediate columns. To raise Pcr​: increase I (tube instead of bar), shorten L, brace the middle or restrain the ends.

Q1 — Simply supported beam, UDL (easy)

A simply supported beam of span 5 m, EI=16000 kN·m² (E=200 GPa, I=80×106 mm⁴), carries a UDL of 12 kN/m. Find the central deflection and the end slope, and compare with L/360.

Hint

M=2wLx​−2wx2​, integrate twice with v(0)=v(L)=0, or use the standard result.

Solution

Take v upward, so EIv′′=M=2w​(Lx−x2). Integrate twice and use v(0)=v(L)=0: EIv=24w​(2Lx3−x4−L3x). At x=L/2 this is v=−384EI5wL4​ (downward), and the end slope has magnitude 24EIwL3​. ymax​=384(16000)5(12)(5)4​=6.10 mm, θ=24(16000)12(125)​=3.91×10−3 rad =0.224∘. L/ymax​=819, comfortably within the L/360 limit (13.9 mm).

Q2 — Cantilever with a load at mid-span (moment-area) (easy–medium)

A cantilever of length 3 m, EI=12000 kN·m², carries 10 kN at mid-span. Find the slope and deflection at the free end.

Hint

Beyond the load the cantilever is unloaded and straight. Slope at the load, then deflection there, then add the straight run.

Solution

With a=1.5 m: θa​=2EIPa2​=2(12000)10(2.25)​=9.375×10−4 rad. This slope is the slope at the tip too. ya​=3EIPa3​=0.9375 mm. Tip: ytip​=ya​+θa​(L−a)=0.9375+1.406=2.34 mm. Closed form 48EI5PL3​=2.34 mm ✓. Moment-area: the area of M/EI is the triangle 21​⋅EIPa​⋅a; its moment about the tip, with centroid a/3 from the load plus a, gives the same.

Q3 — Overhanging beam, tip deflection (medium)

A beam ABC of span AB=4 m rests on supports at A and B and overhangs to C by 1.5 m. A 20 kN load acts at C. EI=12000 kN·m². Find the deflection at C and at mid-span.

Hint

Treat the overhang as a cantilever of length a on a rotating support. The moment M0​=Pa at B bends the span, so the span rises.

Solution

M0​=20(1.5)=30 kN·m. Span AB under an end moment: θB​=3EIM0​L​=3(12000)30(4)​=3.33×10−3 rad. Tip: yC​=θB​a+3EIPa3​=5.00+1.875=6.88 mm down. Mid-span: y=−16EIM0​L2​=−2.50 mm, i.e. the span moves up 2.5 mm. Numerical integration of EIv′′=M gives 6.876 mm and −2.500 mm ✓.

Q4 — Propped cantilever with a UDL (medium–hard)

A cantilever of length 6 m, fixed at A, carries 15 kN/m and is propped at the free end B (rigid prop). Find the reactions, the maximum positive bending moment and its location, the point of contraflexion, and where the deflection is largest.

Hint

One redundant: remove the prop and demand that its deflection from the UDL, wL4/8EI, equals that from RB​, RB​L3/3EI.

Solution

3EIRB​L3​=8EIwL4​ gives RB​=83wL​=33.75 kN, RA​=90−33.75=56.25 kN, MA​=8wL2​=67.5 kN·m (hogging). M(x)=−67.5+56.25x−7.5x2 from A: zero at x=1.5 m (=L/4), the point of contraflexion. V=0 at x=3.75 m from A (=5L/8): Mmax+​=1289wL2​=37.97 kN·m. Largest deflection where θ=0: x=0.5785L=3.47 m from the wall (checked by integration: 3.471 m), with ymax​≈wL4/185EI. Check: the propped cantilever is statically indeterminate to the first degree: the moment at the wall is 2.25 times the maximum span moment, so design for the hogging moment at A.

Q5 — Macaulay's method with two loads (medium–hard)

A simply supported beam of span 8 m carries a 30 kN point load at 3 m from A and a UDL of 10 kN/m from 5 m to 8 m. EI=40000 kN·m². Find the reactions, the deflection at 4 m and the position and size of the maximum deflection.

Hint

M=RA​x−30⟨x−3⟩−5⟨x−5⟩2. Integrate twice, v(0)=0 gives C2​=0, v(8)=0 gives C1​.

Solution

RB​=830(3)+30(6.5)​=35.625 kN, RA​=24.375 kN. With v upward, EIv′′=M, so EIv=6RA​x3​−5⟨x−3⟩3−125​⟨x−5⟩4+C1​x (and C2​=0 from v(0)=0). v(8)=0 gives C1​=−177.66 kN·m². Slope at A: C1​/EI=−4.44×10−3 rad (the beam leaves A sloping downward). At 4 m: v=−11.39 mm, i.e. 11.39 mm downward. The slope is zero at x=3.97 m, where the maximum deflection is 11.39 mm (essentially the same as at 4 m, because the maximum is so flat). Check: a numerical double integration of M(x)/EI gives 11.391 mm at 4 m and a maximum of 11.392 mm ✓.

Q6 — Castigliano on an L-shaped frame (medium)

A frame has a vertical column of height h=2 m fixed at the base and a horizontal arm of length a=1.5 m. A load P=5 kN acts vertically downward at the free end of the arm. EI=8000 kN·m² throughout. Find the vertical deflection of the load point (bending only).

Hint

Write M in each member in terms of P, then δ=∂U/∂P=∑∫EIM​∂P∂M​dx.

Solution

Arm (from the tip): M=Px, ∂M/∂P=x. Column: M=Pa (constant), ∂M/∂P=a. δ=EI1​[∫0a​Px2dx+∫0h​Pa2dy]=3EIPa3​+EIPa2h​. =3(8000)5(3.375)​+80005(2.25)(2)​=0.703+2.813=3.52 mm. The column contributes four times the arm: the column rotates by Pah/EI and the arm lever amplifies it. Neglected: axial energy in the column, Ph/AE, which is about 10−3 of the total for a slender section.

Q7 — Impact on a beam (medium)

A mass of 20 kg falls 100 mm onto the middle of a simply supported steel beam, 40 mm square and 1 m long (E=200 GPa), striking it from rest. Find the maximum deflection and stress, and compare with the static values. Neglect the beam's mass.

Hint

k=48EI/L3, δst​=W/k, then δmax​=δst​(1+1+2h/δst​​). Stress follows from the equivalent static force kδmax​.

Solution

I=404/12=2.133×105 mm⁴, S=403/6=10667 mm³, k=1000348(200000)(2.133×105)​=2048 N/mm. W=196.2 N. δst​=0.0958 mm. δmax​=0.0958(1+1+0.09582(100)​​)=4.47 mm, an impact factor n=46.7. F=kδmax​=9.16 kN, M=FL/4=2.29 kN·m, σ=106672.29×106​=215 MPa (static: 4.6 MPa). Energy check: W(h+δ)=21​kδ2 gives δ=4.474 mm ✓. Insight: a 20 kg mass that is harmless when set down gently stresses the bar to 86 % of yield when dropped 10 cm. A softer beam (smaller k) lowers n and the stress.

Q8 — Euler or not? A tube with four end conditions (medium–hard)

A steel tube, 60 mm outside diameter and 4 mm wall, is 3 m long (E=200 GPa, σY​=250 MPa). Find the critical load for pinned–pinned, fixed–pinned, fixed–fixed and fixed–free ends, using the correct formula in each case, and the allowable load with FS=2.5.

Hint

Compute r and λ=KL/r first. Euler holds only if λ≥λc​=π2E/σY​​.

Solution

A=703.7 mm², I=2.773×105 mm⁴, r=19.85 mm, λc​=125.7, yield load σY​A=175.9 kN.

Ends K λ Euler Pcr​ (kN) Governing formula Pcr​ (kN) Pall​ (kN)
pinned–pinned 1 151 60.8 Euler (λ>λc​) 60.8 24.3
fixed–pinned 0.70 106 124.5 Johnson 113.8 45.5
fixed–fixed 0.5 75.6 243.2 Johnson 144.1 57.6
fixed–free 2 302 15.2 Euler 15.2 6.1

Euler would over-estimate the fixed–fixed column by 69 %: its σcr​=346 MPa is above the yield stress. Johnson gives σcr​=250[1−250(75.6)2/(4π2⋅200000)]=204.8 MPa. Lesson: clamping the ends raises the load by about 2.4 (not 4) because the column becomes intermediate, and slenderness, not the end condition alone, decides the formula.

Q9 — Euler, Johnson and Rankine compared (medium)

A steel column (E=200 GPa, σY​=250 MPa) has radius of gyration r=20 mm. Find the critical stress for effective lengths 1.2, 1.6, 2.4 and 4.0 m using Euler and Johnson (and Rankine with σc​=330 MPa, a=1/7500), and say which governs.

Hint

λ=Le​/r, with transition at λc​=125.7.

Solution
Le​ (m) λ Euler (MPa) Johnson (MPa) Rankine (MPa) governs
1.2 60 548 221.5 223.0 Johnson
1.6 80 308 199.3 178.1 Johnson
2.4 120 137 136.0 113.0 Johnson
4.0 200 49.3 (negative: invalid) 52.1 Euler

At λc​ the two curves meet at σY​/2=125 MPa with equal slope. Rankine is a single empirical formula that is safe (lower) in the intermediate range and approaches Euler at large λ; its constants are material-specific. Lesson: never apply Euler without checking λ first. At λ=60 the Euler value is 2.5× the true capacity.

Q10 — Eccentric column: the secant formula (hard)

A pinned–pinned steel column of length 2.4 m has a 100×60 mm section and carries a load at an eccentricity e=6 mm in the 60 mm direction (bending about the weak axis). E=200 GPa, σY​=250 MPa. Find the load at which the maximum stress reaches yield, and compare with the Euler load and with a linear estimate.

Hint

σmax​=AP​[1+r2ec​sec(2rL​EAP​​)]; solve numerically for σmax​=σY​.

Solution

A=6000 mm², I=1.8×106 mm⁴, r2=300 mm², c=30 mm, ec/r2=0.6, λ=138.6, PE​=L2π2EI​=616.9 kN. Solving σmax​(P)=250 MPa gives P=437 kN. Linear estimate ignoring the extra moment Pv: P=1+ec/r2σY​A​=937.5 kN: more than twice too high. Yield load without eccentricity: 1500 kN. At half that load (218 kN) σmax​=73 MPa; doubling the load more than triples the stress, because the deflection grows as P→PE​. Lesson: for eccentric columns the allowable load is set by yielding well below PE​, and the stress grows faster than the load, so apply the factor of safety to the load, not to the stress.
