Moody Chart

conceptfluid-mechanicspipe-flowmoody-chartfriction-factor

← up: Fluid Mechanics MOC · builds on: Darcy–Weisbach Equation and Friction Factor, Laminar and Turbulent Flow · formulas: Pipe Bank §3

Core Idea

One-sentence definition: the Moody chart is a log–log plot of the Darcy friction factor f against the Reynolds number for a family of relative roughnesses e/D, combining the laminar law and the Colebrook turbulent law in one picture.

Intuition: it shows when the pipe wall's roughness matters. At low Re (laminar) the wall is irrelevant. At high Re the viscous sublayer becomes thinner than the roughness bumps, and the pipe behaves as "fully rough": friction depends on roughness only, not on speed.

Governing Relations

The curves are:

f=Re64​ (Re<2300),f​1​=−2.0log10​(3.7e/D​+Ref​2.51​) (Re>4000),frough​=[−2.0log10​3.7e/D​]−2

Regions of the chart:

Region Re Behaviour
laminar <2300 one straight line, slope −1 on log–log axes; independent of e/D
critical (transition) zone 2300–4000 f jumps and is uncertain; avoid designing here
smooth-pipe curve any, e/D→0 lowest turbulent f, falls slowly with Re (Blasius 0.3164Re−1/4 for Re<105)
transition-rough intermediate f depends on both Re and e/D
fully rough large Re each e/D curve flattens horizontally: f=f(e/D) only, so Δp∝V2 exactly

Smooth or rough? A pipe is hydraulically smooth when e≲δv​≈5ν/uτ​ (the viscous sublayer thickness) and fully rough when e≳60ν/uτ​ (roughly), with uτ​=Vf/8​.

Where: e = equivalent sand-grain roughness (mm); e/D = relative roughness; ν = kinematic viscosity.

Assumptions: fully developed turbulent flow in a circular pipe with uniform roughness (commercial pipes are covered by the Colebrook equation, which interpolates the transition region smoothly).

When To Use

  • Reading f quickly from Re and e/D.
  • Judging how much roughness matters (is the pipe near the smooth or the fully rough limit?).
  • Deciding whether Δp∝V2 (fully rough) or ∝V1.75-ish (smooth) when scaling a flow.

Recognition pattern: "from the Moody chart", "relative roughness", "fully rough turbulent flow" → Moody chart.

Method

  1. Identify Re=VD/ν and e/D (look up e).
  2. Choose the region: laminar line, smooth curve, or an e/D curve.
  3. Apply: read f at the point; for accuracy use Haaland (explicit) or iterate Colebrook.
  4. Check: that Re>4000 for the turbulent curves, and compare against the fully rough value as a sanity bound (f≥frough​ and f≥fsmooth​).

Derivation / Why It Works

Smooth limit. Near the wall the viscous sublayer of thickness δv​∼ν/uτ​ covers roughness bumps, so only viscous scales matter and f=f(Re). Rough limit. If the bumps stick out of the sublayer, the drag is pressure (form) drag on the roughness elements, proportional to ρV2 and independent of ν, so f=f(e/D). The Colebrook equation is the empirical formula that joins the two limits: it is 1/f​=−2log(rough term+smooth term).

Common Traps

  • Trap: reading the laminar branch for roughness effects: there are none.
  • Trap: reading the chart in the critical zone and trusting the value.
  • Trap: using Fanning-factor charts (values are 1/4 of Darcy).
  • Trap: using e instead of e/D to select the curve.
  • Trap: assuming f is constant: it is only constant in the fully rough region.

Example

Given

The 100 mm commercial-steel pipe of the previous note: e/D=4.6×10−4, Re=1.26×105 with f=0.0196. How does f change when the flow is raised to Re=2.5×105 and 106? What is the fully rough limit?

Solution

Colebrook iteration gives:

Re f
1.26×105 0.0196
2.5×105 0.0182
106 0.0169
∞ (fully rough) 0.0164
smooth pipe at 1.26×105 0.0172

Check of the regime: δv​=5ν/uτ​=5(1.01×10−6)/0.063=0.08 mm against e=0.046 mm: the roughness is inside the sublayer, so this pipe is nearly smooth at this Re; it only becomes fully rough at much higher Re.

Answer: f falls from 0.0196 to 0.0182 and 0.0169, tending to 0.0164. Key takeaway: at higher speed f falls only 14 % over an 8-fold range of Re, so Δp rises almost as V2 (roughly V1.93).
