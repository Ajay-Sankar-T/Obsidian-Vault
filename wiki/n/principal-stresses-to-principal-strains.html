The pattern (Q5)

Given the principal stresses σ1​,σ2​,σ3​ at a point (found via Plane Stress Transformation/Mohr's circle, or given directly), find the corresponding principal strains.

At a principal-stress orientation, the shear stresses vanish by definition — and because Hooke's law has no coupling between normal stress and shear strain for an isotropic material (see Generalized Hooke's Law and Poisson's Ratio's uncoupled shear equations γij​=τij​/G), the strain in that same orientation also has zero shear component. The principal directions of stress and strain coincide for an isotropic material — this is why it's valid to plug principal stresses directly into the normal-strain Hooke's law equations without any transformation step:

ε1​=E1​[σ1​−ν(σ2​+σ3​)]​,ε2​=E1​[σ2​−ν(σ1​+σ3​)]​,ε3​=E1​[σ3​−ν(σ1​+σ2​)]​

​ε1​ε2​ε3​​​=E1​​1−ν−ν​−ν1−ν​−ν−ν1​​​σ1​σ2​σ3​​​​

The important subtlety

A compressive principal stress does not necessarily produce a compressive principal strain in that direction — the Poisson contributions from the other two principal stresses can outweigh it and flip the sign. E.g. if σ1​<0 but σ2​,σ3​>0 and large, ε1​=E1​[σ1​−ν(σ2​+σ3​)] can still come out negative more than expected, or in the reverse scenario a small compressive σ1​ combined with strongly tensile σ2​,σ3​ can even push ε1​ positive. Always compute — never assume the sign of εi​ matches the sign of σi​ in a multiaxial state.

Why this matters: it's the same eigenbasis, twice

This coincidence of principal directions is the isotropic-material special case of a more general linear-algebra fact explored in Eigenvalues, Rotation and Diagonalization Everywhere: the stress tensor [σ] and strain tensor [ε] are related by the isotropic compliance tensor (the matrix above), which for an isotropic material is itself diagonal in every orthonormal basis (it commutes with any rotation). Two matrices that commute are always simultaneously diagonalizable — which is precisely why [σ] and [ε] share the same eigenvectors (principal directions) here. This would not hold for an anisotropic (e.g. composite/fibre-reinforced) material, where the compliance tensor is not isotropic and the principal strain directions can rotate away from the principal stress directions.
