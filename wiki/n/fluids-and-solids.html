The defining distinction

The line between a solid and a fluid is drawn by how each responds to a shear (tangential) stress in static equilibrium:

Solid: can resist a tangential force while remaining in static equilibrium — it deforms a finite amount and (fully or partly) recovers its shape when the load is removed.

Fluid: cannot sustain any shear stress in static equilibrium — under any nonzero shear, it deforms continuously (it flows) for as long as the stress is applied, and generally does not recover its original shape when the load is removed. This is the "zero memory" behavior of an ordinary fluid, with the exception of viscoelastic fluids (see Newtonian and Non-Newtonian Fluids) which retain some elastic memory of past deformation.

Why this is the foundational definition

Every later topic in this vault traces back to this one distinction:

  • Fluid statics is possible as a subject at all only because a fluid at rest carries no shear stress anywhere — the entire stress state at a point reduces to a single scalar, pressure, acting equally in all directions (in stark contrast to the full stress tensor [σ] needed for a solid — see Concept of Stress in the Strength of Materials vault, and the cross-domain note below).
  • Viscosity is precisely the quantitative version of "how does a fluid resist shear once it's moving" — a fluid can't resist shear statically, but it does resist the rate of shearing once flowing.

Cross-domain: a fluid at rest is a degenerate solid

A useful way to unify this with Concept of Stress: a fluid's stress state, [σ]=−p[I] (pressure acting equally in all directions, no off-diagonal shear terms), is the isotropic special case of the general stress tensor used throughout the Strength of Materials vault. Mohr's Circle for Stress degenerates to a single point on the σ-axis at σ=−p for a fluid at rest — there is no principal-stress problem to solve, because every direction is already principal (the circle has radius zero). This is exactly why hydrostatic pressure needs no "transformation equations": FR​=ρghc​A in Hydrostatic Force on Plane Surfaces doesn't care about the orientation of the surface's own coordinate axes, only its depth.
