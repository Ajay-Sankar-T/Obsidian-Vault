T3-Q2: RMS of a Staircase Waveform, and the Power It Dissipates

EE1100tutorial-solutionAC-circuitsrmswaveforms

Hub: Tutorial 3 - AC Circuit Analysis · Theory: Sinusoidal Waveforms, RMS and Average Value · Previous: T3-Q1 - Average and RMS of Square Waves · Next: T3-Q3 - RL Series Circuit Impedance

A voltage wave steps through 0→10→20 V (each for 1 s, repeating every 3 s). Find the average and rms values; if applied to 10 Ω, the power dissipated. [Sheet: Vavg​=10 V, Vrms​=1015​/3=12.909 V, P=16.667 W]

Tests: rms of a piecewise-constant wave, power in a resistor from the rms value.

Given

Period T=3 s: v=0 on 0<t<1, v=10 V on 1<t<2, v=20 V on 2<t<3. Load R=10 Ω.

Principle and why it applies

For a piecewise-constant waveform the integrals reduce to sums over segments weighted by their duration. The power in a resistor depends on the mean square, P=RVrms2​​, which is exactly what the rms value packages. (The average value is only the DC component, not the heating.)

Diagram

Solution

Vavg​=30⋅1+10⋅1+20⋅1​=10 V,

Vrms2​=302⋅1+102⋅1+202⋅1​=3500​=166.67 V2 ⇒ Vrms​=166.67​=12.91 V=31015​​.

P=RVrms2​​=10166.67​=16.67 W.

Result

Vavg​=10 V,Vrms​=12.91 V,P=16.67 W​

Check

Time-average of the instantaneous power: T1​∑Rv2​Δt=31​[100​+10100​+10400​]=350​=16.67 W ✓. And Vrms2​=Vavg2​+σ2 with variance σ2=31​[(−10)2+0+102]=66.7: 100+66.7=166.7 ✓.

Trap / alternative

Using Vavg2​/R=100/10=10 W for the power is the classic error: it ignores the AC component and underestimates the heating (10 W against 16.67 W). Likewise (0+10+20)/3​ is not the rms. Always average the squares.
